Preface to the Guiguzi鬼谷子序

Qin Enfu 秦恩復 of Jiangdu　·　Jiaqing 10 (1805)

The Guiguzi does not appear in the bibliographical treatise of the History of the Han; it is first catalogued under the Sui and the Tang. Both the Old and the New History of the Tang take it for a work of Su Qin. Yet among the Vertical-and-Horizontal writers the History of the Han lists a separate Master Su in thirty-two chapters, whose prose is nothing like the Guiguzi; and if Su Qin had in fact published under a borrowed name, why should Ban Gu have passed the matter over without a word? Lu Guimeng holds that Guiguzi was one Wang Xu. Wang Jia's Records of Gleanings holds that Ghost Valley (鬼谷) is Returning Valley (歸谷), gui "ghost" and gui "return" being no more than a shift of sound — and indeed the Erya glosses: "gui, ghost, is to say gui, to return."

The claim that Su Qin wrote under a borrowed name rests on this much: that Zhang Yi and Su Qin studied under Guiguzi, and that Su Qin's biography in the Records of the Grand Historian has the phrase "he drilled himself in estimating and probing," while the Guiguzi happens to contain two chapters called Estimating and Probing. On that the theory was built; in truth there is nothing to support it. Others say he was a man of spirit of the Zhou age who lived hidden in Ghost Valley — and this comes nearer the mark.

The book runs to three scrolls. From Opening and Closing to Words as Tallies there are twelve chapters; the two chapters Turning the Pellet and Prying the Chest were lost long ago. To these are added the Seven Arts of the Yin Tally of the Root Canon, together with Holding the Pivot and the Middle Canon — twenty-one chapters in all. Liu Zongyuan once mocked the book as devious and steep, harsh and thin, wild talk that throws an age into disorder. But look at it now: it picks out what lies hidden, turns and doubles back and changes shape. Su Qin got no more than its leavings, and with one wag of his tongue made himself chief of the Vertical Alliance. It is truly the ancestor of the Vertical-and-Horizontal school.

Checking the passages quoted in the Garden of Persuasions, in the commentaries to the Records of the Grand Historian and the Wenxuan, in the Yilin and the Imperial Readings of the Taiping Era: some are not to be found in the book as we have it, and some differ in wording from present copies. After the disorder of the Five Dynasties the text, passing from hand to hand by copying, gradually lost its true shape — 陶 written for 陰, 帝 for 虎 — corruptions and omissions following one upon another, and not in the case of Turning the Pellet and Prying the Chest alone.

As for those who annotated the Guiguzi, there were formerly three: Yue Yi, Huangfu Mi and Yin Zhizhang. One note of Yue's survives in the commentary to the Wenxuan; several items in the Taiping Yulan likewise carry no commentator's name. The Zhongxing Catalogue is the first to list a commentary by Tao Hongjing, and Chao Gongwu and Chen Zhensun followed it. Now Zhenbai — Tao Hongjing — lived under the Liang of the Xiao house, yet the book surfaced late, and readers are not without their suspicions. My contemporary Zhou Gengya of Haining, the licentiate, concluded from the frequent avoidance of Tang taboo-names in the commentary that it is Yin's work and not Tao's; his argument is wide and eloquent, but it too is conjecture resting on air, without a shred of corroboration. Only Ma Duanlin, under "Tao's commentary" in the Comprehensive Examination of Documents, says: "The Tang bibliography takes it for Yin Zhizhang's commentary; which is right is not known." So already in Song times the two accounts were kept side by side. Happily, under the name of the True Recluse of Huayang, the book found shelter in the Daoist Canon; and whether it be Tao's or Yin's, one may decide with certainty that it is no work of post-Song date.

This book was cut in the jiyou year of Qianlong (1789), collated only against the copy recorded by Circuit Intendant Sun Xingyan at the Mount Hua temple. Master Lu Wenchao went over the collation again, and again, and a third time; and at the last he sent me by post the old Shugutang manuscript, from which I learned for the first time how many corruptions and omissions the Daoist Canon text in fact preserves. Reading books is hard indeed; collating them is no easier.

I have therefore committed it to the blocks afresh, taking the Qian copy as the basis throughout. Where the Canon copy has what the Qian copy lacks, I have examined the differences and checked the one against the other. I have further culled the old commentary quoted in Tang and Song books and notes, gathering and preserving it below the text. And such matter as is lost from present copies but survives elsewhere, together with accounts of Guiguzi's doings that may serve for reference, I have appended at the end, for the reader's convenience.

Jiaqing 10, yichou, the fifteenth day of the eighth month (1805). Prefaced by Qin Enfu of Jiangdu.

1. Opening and Closing捭闔第一

Bai is to pry open, to stir into motion; he is to shut and store away. The chapter is the frame for everything that follows: all dealing with men reduces to drawing them out or shutting oneself in.

To open is to stir into motion; to close is to shut and store away. In all speaking with others one either stirs a man so that he speaks, and so shows him agreement; or shuts oneself away so that he speaks of himself, and so shows him difference.

Now, looking back to antiquity: the sage, dwelling between heaven and earth, Yue is "to follow"; ji is "to examine." The sage, dwelling between heaven and earth, observes men and sets up his teaching, and must follow and examine the ancient Way in doing so. is forerunner to the living multitude. He issues first out of the mass of things; being the earlier man, he uses what he knows first to wake those who know later, and what he perceives first to wake those who perceive later. Hence he is forerunner to the multitude. He watches the opening and closing of yin and yang, and by names calls things into their names; Yang opens and so gives things birth; yin closes and so brings them to completion. Birth and completion once manifest, names must be set up to designate them. he knows the gate of survival and ruin; He who does not forget ruin survives; he who takes his survival for granted perishes. Who can know fortune and disaster before they appear, but the man who knows the incipient? Hence: "he knows the gate of survival and ruin." he reckons out the ends and beginnings of the ten thousand kinds, penetrates the pattern of the human heart, and sees the first traces of change; The ends and beginnings of the ten thousand kinds, the pattern of the human heart, the first traces of change — there is none he does not grasp in deep and lucid understanding, no obscurity he cannot sound; hence he can reckon, and see far. Zhen means "trace." and he guards and keeps that gate. "The gate" is the gate of survival and ruin above. The sage, having penetrated the ends and beginnings of the pattern of things and known the gate of survival and ruin, can guard and keep it, making men turn their backs on ruin and hasten toward survival. ○ Collation: in the Daoist Canon copy the note has four more graphs before 門戶: 「司，主守也」.

So the sage in the world: from antiquity down ○ Collation: for 及 the Daoist Canon copy has 之, the Bao copy 至. to now, his Way is one. None but turns his back on ruin and hastens toward survival; hence "his Way is one." Its transformations are without end, yet each has that to which it returns; Though the Way is one, the practice of it differs — hence "transformations without end." Yet it is ordered and not tangled — hence "each has that to which it returns." now yin, now yang; now yielding, now hard; now open, now shut; now slack, now drawn taut. This says that the figures and models differ, and that the teaching applied is not the same. Therefore the sage keeps single ○ Collation: the Yilin has no 一. guard over that gate and examines closely what ○ Collation: the Yilin quotation has no 所. comes first and what after. Governments and teachings differ, but in guarding the gate they are one; so he examines what ought to come first and does it first, what ought to come after and does it after. He gauges their cunning and measures their capacity, and weighs the long and the short of their skills. Quan means stratagem; neng, ability; jiqiao, the work of the hundred crafts. In employing men the sage must gauge whether their stratagem and ability are fine or poor and check whether their skills run long or short, and then appoint them according to the stuff they are made of.

Worthy and unworthy, wise and foolish, brave and timid, humane and righteous — these differ by degree; and so one may open, or close; advance a man, or set him back; hold him cheap, or hold him dear; and shepherd them all by doing nothing. Worthy and unworthy, wise and foolish, brave and timid differ in stuff and nature, each having its grade. To the worthy one may open and treat him as of one's own kind; against the unworthy one may close and hold him other. The wise and the brave may be advanced and entrusted; the foolish and the timid set back and held cheap. Let worthy and foolish each sit in his own station and every limb spend its own strength: the ruler need only compose himself and do nothing — shepherd them, and no more. He settles what they have and have not, and ○ Collation: the Daoist Canon copy has 以. what in them is solid and what hollow; he follows their appetites and desires, so as to see their aims and intents. In the way of appointing worthy men one must settle whether they have talent and art or not, whether nature and conduct are solid or hollow, and only then appoint them in accordance with their appetites, so as to see whether their aims are true or false. He pushes back lightly against what they say and pries it open against them, so as to get at the fact; what matters is to seize their drift. He closes, and then opens, so as to find where their advantage lies. Whenever a minister speaks on a matter, the ruler lightly pushes his words back and holds them down, stirs him, puts the objection to him, and so reaches the true state of things. Having that, he shuts himself away and stirs the other, so as to find out what advantage the man's words are meant to serve.

Sometimes he opens and shows himself; sometimes he closes and shuts himself in. To open and show is to make common cause with their feeling; to close and shut is to stand apart and try their good faith. By opening and making common cause he draws out the whole of their feeling; by closing and standing apart he learns how sincere they are. Feasible and unfeasible: he examines their plans clearly and traces them back to where they agree and differ. In what a minister says there is the feasible and the unfeasible; one must examine his plans clearly and trace back their agreement and difference. ○ Collation: for 凡臣 the Daoist Canon copy has 凡有. Whether they part from him or join him, so long as they hold to something he first goes along with their intent. Though their plans differ in parting or joining, so long as they hold fast to something he first follows their intent and lets it run its full course, and so learns where success and failure will fall.

If you would open, what counts is thoroughness; if you would close, what counts is secrecy. In thoroughness and secrecy alike what counts is subtlety, ○ Collation: the Wenxuan commentary quotes it as 「即欲聞之貴密，密之貴㣲」. and so to keep pace with the Way. In stirring, what counts is that it reach everywhere; in shutting away, that it be hidden and close. Both must be subtle and fine and match the pattern of the Way — only then are they got right. Opening is for sorting their feelings; closing is for binding up their good faith. Liao is to sift and choose; jie is to tie and bind. Feeling may be true or false, so it must be sifted; good faith may not hold to the end, so it must be bound fast. In every case one sees where they stand on the balance, light or heavy, and then fixes measure and number for them; and the sage lays his plans for them accordingly. The balance once set out, light and heavy divide of themselves; then measure and number are fixed, to govern the light and the heavy. Each being in its place, plans are laid accordingly, and they are made to follow them. For those who do not answer to balance, measure and number, the sage lays his plans for himself instead. If a thing's weight does not match the catty and the ounce, if its length does not fill out the measure, it is refuse — what use is to be made of it? So the sage plans for himself, and looks instead for the opposite.

So of opening: one may open and send out, or open and take ○ Collation: for 內 the Daoist Canon copy has 納; in old writing 內 serves for 納. in. Those who answer to the balance are sent out and employed; those who do not are taken in and stored away. And of closing: one may close and take, or close and let go. Upon the sincere he closes and takes; upon the insincere he closes and lets go.

Opening and closing are the Way of heaven and earth. "To shut the door is called Kun; to swing the door open is called Qian" — hence "the Way of heaven and earth." By opening and closing, yin and yang are shifted and moved and the four seasons open and shut, and so the ten thousand things are transformed, raised up and put down; The shifting of yin and yang and the opening and shutting of the four seasons are all the way of opening and closing. Zongheng means putting down and raising up: the ten thousand things are now opened and so raised, now closed and so put down. turning out, turning back, turning athwart — all must come from this. In the way of opening and closing, one may turn a thing so that it issues from the other side, or turn it so that it comes back to this side, or turn it toward the other and set it crosswise here: all are born of opening and closing. Hence "must come from this." Opening and closing are the great transformation of the Way and the turning of persuasion; one must examine their changes beforehand, Had affairs no opening and closing, the great Way would not transform and speech would not turn. So opening and closing are what transforms the great Way and turns persuasion. However great a matter, it is brought to completion through transformation; hence one must examine beforehand. for on them hang fortune, disaster and the great mandate. By the mandate of heaven is meant the sage's receiving heaven's charge and ruling the world; yet this too arises out of transformation. Hence "on them hang fortune, disaster and the great mandate." ○ Collation: the Daoist Canon copy lacks both this text and this note.

The mouth is the gate of the heart; the heart is the master of the spirit. The heart is uttered by the mouth — hence "the mouth is the gate of the heart." The spirit is put to use by the heart — hence "the heart is the master of the spirit." Aim and intent, delight and desire, thought and deliberation, wit and stratagem: all pass in and out ○ Collation: the Yilin has 「智謀皆從之出」. by that gate. All eight of these come and go by way of the mouth; hence "pass in and out by the gate." So bar it with opening and closing, and govern it by going out and coming in. Opening is swinging wide, is speech, is yang. Closing is shutting, is silence, is yin. Had these eight no opening and shutting, affairs would run unregulated; so barring them with opening and closing is the means of governing their going out and coming in. Speech opened outward is called yang; feeling shut within is called yin. Let yin and yang be in accord, and end and beginning be each in its right sense. Opening and shutting being regulated, yin and yang accord; before and after being fitting, end and beginning are right.

So talk of long life, ease and joy, wealth and rank, honour and glory, bright fame, ○ Collation: one copy has 「榮顯名譽」. love and liking, goods and profit, getting one's wish, delight and desire — this is yang, and is called the beginning. All of these wish a man life; hence "beginning," hence "yang." And talk of death and ruin, grief and trouble, poverty and low estate, bitterness and disgrace, being cast off and cut down, losing profit, missing one's wish, harm, mutilation and execution, punishment and penalty — this is yin, and is called the end. All of these wish a man death; hence "yin," hence "end." All speech patterned on the yang kind is called beginning: one speaks of the good to begin the affair. All speech patterned on the yin kind is called end: one speaks of the bad to finish off the design. A persuader may speak in the yang manner or in the yin; the hearer had better know which it is.

The way of opening and closing tries men by yin and yang. Now stirring him, now shutting away, one tries a man with yin and yang speech, and what he leans toward can be known. So with one who speaks in the yang manner, rest on the lofty and high; with one who speaks in the yin manner, rest on the low and small. To one of yang temper speak resting on the lofty, and so draw him out; to one of yin temper speak resting on the low, and so draw him out. By the low one seeks the small; by the high one seeks the great. Yin speech is low and small — hence "by the low one seeks the small." Yang speech is lofty and high — hence "by the high one seeks the great." Speaking from this, there is nowhere one cannot go out, nowhere one cannot come in, nothing one cannot do. The pattern of yin and yang being exhausted and the states of small and great obtained, going out and coming in are alike possible; what then is not possible? One may persuade a man, persuade a house, persuade a state, persuade the world. Nothing being impossible, every persuasion is possible. Made small, it has no inside; made great, it has no outside. Yin carried to the end has no inside; yang carried to the end has no outside.

Increase and decrease, leaving and joining, turning away and turning back — all drive the matter by yin and yang. To complete one another by the Way is increase; to injure one another through affairs is decrease. When right conduct diverges, that is leaving; when intents agree, that is joining. To leave and break off for good is turning away; to leave and come back again is turning back. None of this falls outside the states of yin and yang; hence "all drive the matter by yin and yang." Yang moves and proceeds, yin halts and stores away; yang moves and goes out, yin hides and comes in; yang, coming round, ends in yin, and yin at its utmost turns back into yang. This says that ruler and minister complete one another as yin and yang give birth to one another. ○ Collation: for 君臣 the Daoist Canon copy has 上下, and so throughout below. Moving by yang, they give birth to one another through virtue; stilled by yin, they complete one another through form. Seeking yin by yang, one enfolds them in virtue; binding yang by yin, one spends one's strength on them. This says that the ruler nourishes the ministers below him with rank and emolument, and the minister spends his strength as arm and thigh. That yin and yang seek one another comes of opening and closing. What lets ruler and minister seek one another is born of opening and shutting.

This is the Way of heaven and earth, of yin and yang; and it is the method of persuading men. Since it takes heaven and earth for its body and yin and yang for its figure, its method can be used to persuade men. It goes before the ten thousand affairs; it is called the gate of the round and the square. Heaven is round and earth square: such is the sense of ruler and minister. Only when the pattern of opening and shutting is complete are the ten thousand things born; hence it goes before the ten thousand affairs. The way of ruler and minister passes in and out by it; hence "the gate of the round and the square." The round is the ruler; the square is the minister. ○ Collation: the Daoist Canon copy lacks the six graphs 「圓，君也；方，臣也」.

2. Turning Back and Responding反應第二

To learn what a man has in him, contradict him. Push his words back and he must think again; and in thinking again he answers, and in answering he shows himself.

In the way of listening to speech, where something does not fit one must turn it back and press the objection. The other, pressed, thinks again, and is bound to have some response. ○ Collation: the Taiping Yulan cites it as 《反覆篇》 ("Turning and Returning"); on the evidence of the text itself that should be the title.

The great transformers of antiquity were born together with the formless. By "great transformers" are meant the sages of antiquity, who transformed things by the great Way. The formless is the Way: since all their motion followed the Way, it is said they were "born together with the formless." They turned back to observe what had gone, and returned to verify what was coming; turned back to know antiquity, and returned to know the present; turned back to know the other, and returned to know the self. The great transforming sage consults the many and sets his own view aside; he takes a matter up with weight and care, turning and returning, verifying in detail. Wishing to know what is coming, he first observes what has gone; wishing to know the present, he first examines antiquity; wishing to know himself, he first takes the measure of others. So no plan of his is ever left wanting, and every move succeeds. ○ Collation: the Daoist Canon copy has 覆以知己 ("returns to know the self"); on the evidence of the note, "know the self" is right. Where the pattern of motion and stillness, of the hollow and the solid, does not fit the present, turn back to antiquity and seek it there. Motion and stillness come of going and stopping; hollow and solid, of the true and the false. When their pattern does not fit the present, one turns back and seeks it in antiquity. That a matter should be turned back and so come round — that is the sage's intent, Where a matter does not fit, he turns and seeks it on the other side, and so gets it to come round and meet here. Completing this lies in examining that; squaring with the present comes of seeking in antiquity. Such is the sage's intent. and it must not go unexamined. Failing to examine, one misses the incipient moment; hence it must not go unexamined.

When another speaks, that is motion; when I keep silent, that is stillness. Take his speech, and listen to his words. Observing motion from stillness, what one sees is exact; listening to the wording through the speech, what one gets is clear. Where the speech does not fit, turn it back and seek it out, and his response is certain to come out. A speaker may say what does not fit the pattern; do not reject it at once, but turn it back and press him, and let him seek it for himself — then the response that fits the pattern comes out of itself, and gladly. Speech has its figures; affairs have their parallels. Having figure and parallel, watch what comes next. The response fitting the pattern once out, his speech can have figures and his affairs parallels. The earlier matter having figure and parallel, one should then watch what comes next, and let him say his whole mind. Xiang means model-figure; bi means parallel case. The figure figures the affair; the parallel matches the wording. By the formless one seeks the sounded. The pattern lies in the deep and subtle, hence "formless." Unspoken it is not made plain, hence "by the formless one seeks the sounded" — that is, speech. Bi means matching of kinds. His angled speech fits the case, and one has the man's reality. Getting the fish depends on casting the bait; getting his words depends on starting them off. Start them and the words answer; cast the bait and the fish comes. So "angled speech" makes the matter fit — hence "fits the case." The clear test lies in spreading words before him — hence "one has the man's reality." ○ Collation: in the Daoist Canon copy the note has 曰 before 釣語 and 語 after it. It is like spreading nets to take game: spread many at the runs and keep watch, and when the Way fits the case, the other comes out of himself. This is the net for angling men. Spread the net and keep watch, and the beast delivers itself; let the Way fit the case, and the pattern comes out of itself. The pattern once manifest, sage and worthy are told apart; and though a man would hide himself, he has no way to. Hence "the net for angling men." Hold that net constantly and drive him into it; and if he will not speak, and there is no parallel, then change for his sake. Holding the net for angling men, one drives him to his proper business. He may be at odds with you and so not speak, and there be no parallel; then one changes for his sake — alters the usual, shifts the net, and finds some other way to figure it. ○ Collation: for 象 the Daoist Canon copy has 動; since the text below reads 以象動之, both graphs should stand in the note.

Move him with a figure, to answer to his heart; and once you see his feeling, follow it and shepherd him. This is the changing. Bao is as much as "to meet." One opens some further model-figure to move him with; this once meeting his heart, his feeling can be seen, and one follows that feeling and pastures him on it. I turn toward him and he comes back to me; speech having figure and parallel, on this the foundation is laid. I turn toward him to seek him out, and he must come back and take up his duty; then rare plans are sure to be set forth. So when speech has figure and parallel, the mouth has no words to pick over, and the foundation of a state and a house can be laid on it. Weight it, layer it, turn it, return it, and in ten thousand affairs the wording will not fail. The speech of figure and parallel being enough to lay a foundation, one then weights and layers it, turns and returns it — all meaning: examine it twice and three times, leaving no room for error, so that ten thousand affairs are amply satisfied and the wording never fails again. When the sage draws out the foolish and the wise alike, in neither case is there doubt about the affair. Drawing out the foolish, he shuts himself away, and so learns how sincere they are; drawing out the wise, he stirs them, and so exhausts their feeling. Getting the reality of both, he has no doubt about the affair.

So the man skilled at listening in reverse can turn ghost and spirit themselves, and get at what men feel. He who is skilled at listening in reverse sits in forgetting and lays down the mirror; without thinking he deeply surveys, and so can turn ghost and spirit and get at what men feel, sounding the hidden and subtle and meeting it in the dark. Ghosts and spirits are secret by nature; here they can be so no longer — hence "turn." His changes being apt, his shepherding is exact. The change made and fitting the pattern, the way of shepherding is then exact. Shepherd without exactness and the feeling one gets is unclear; the feeling unclear, the foundation is laid without exactness. Clarity of feeling lies in exact shepherding, so without exactness there is no clarity; exactness of foundation lies in clarity of feeling, so without clarity there is no exactness.

When figure and parallel shift, there must be words turned back, to be listened to in return. If the speaker shifts in figure and parallel, there must be words turned back to press him, so that he speaks, and I go quiet again and listen. ○ Collation: for 有言 the Daoist Canon copy has 先說. Would you hear his voice, be silent instead; would you have him spread out, draw in ○ Collation: the Daoist Canon copy has 瞼. This is the way of listening in reverse: there are means of luring him to it. So to hear the other's voice I go still and silent; to have the other spread out I draw my lids in; to have the other be high and great I go humble and low; to have the other take I give. In this way the state of things can be brought out, and nothing can hide itself. ○ Collation: for 瞼歛 the Qian copy has 歛歛, which looks to be an error; I follow the Daoist Canon copy in correcting it. Would you open up his feeling, set a figure and a parallel before him, and shepherd his words. Like voices call to one another; true patterns come home to the same place. To open the other's feeling, first set out figure and parallel to move him. His feeling once moved, words are about to be born; pasture him slowly and let him speak of himself. It is as when the crane cries in the shade: the like voice must answer, and so true patterns come home together. Now from this side, now from that; now to serve those above, now to shepherd those below. The matter spoken of may be started from this side or from that; and the affair may be such as serves those above or shepherds those below. By this one hears the true and the false, knows sameness and difference, and gets at feeling and deception. True and false, same and different, honest and deceitful are known by means of the foregoing.

Act and rest, speech and silence, go out and come in with this; delight and anger show their form by it. Acting and resting, speaking and keeping silence all proceed from feeling, and go out and come in with it; and delight and anger too show their form through that feeling. Knowing whether a lord's design is able or not, one then sets up a rule to hold down his moving and resting and govern his loosing, as the pivot does for the door and the trigger for the bow; now going before to meet him, now coming after to follow him, in all cases clamping his feeling to bring him into accord and using his own intent to spread it. So the lords' power may be got and held, and one's own kindness and good faith made firm. Hence "the joinery of flying and clamping" — using the art to link oneself to men.

Used upon a person, empty goes out and solid comes back; hold the joint and do not lose it, and so run his words to the end. Then you may clamp him vertical or clamp him horizontal, draw him east or draw him west, draw him south or draw him north, draw him to turn or draw him to turn back. "Used upon a person" means employing men by the art of flying and clamping. I send out nothing but reputation to raise him — hence "empty goes out"; he opens his heart and shows his feeling and comes over to me — hence "solid comes back." Having his feeling, one must hold the joint and not lose it, and further have him lay out his case in words, and so run his words to the end. Then vertical or horizontal, east or west, south or north, turning or turning back, all lies in one's own clamping and drawing, and there is no thought of his that does not submit. Even overturned, he can be set up again, and the measure is not lost. Though there be an overturning and defeat, he can surely be raised up again and not lose his measure. This is the end of flying and clamping.

6. Crossing and Joining忤合第六

In a fallen age no straight road runs straight through. To join yourself to this side you must first cross over to that, so that no one suspects you; only then can you do what you meant to do. Yi Yin and Lü Shang went and came in just this fashion.

The great Way being hidden away, the straight road cannot be walked openly; so if one would join with this side, one must cross over to that, and set them at ease, and only then carry out one's intent — as with the goings and comings of Yi Yin and Lü Shang.

In all hastening to join and turning away to go back, there is a reckoning that fits the case. Hastening to join and turning away to go back are ragged and uneven things; yet applied to design and reckoning, their pattern fits exactly. Transforming and turning, they link like the rings of a chain, each with its own shape and force; they seek one another by turning and returning, and the rule is made from the matter in hand. The pattern of turning away and going back turns as things transform, like linked rings. Yet in leaving and joining each has its shape and force; now turning, now returning, the pattern seeks itself out, and in every case one takes the other's circumstance and makes the rule from it.

So the sage, placed between heaven and earth, standing himself up and driving his age, spreading his teaching and making his name resound, must take the concurrence of things and events, observe what heaven's season makes fitting, and so know what is in excess and what in want; by this he knows beforehand, and turns and transforms with it. "In excess and in want" means what in government and teaching should be more and what less. Knowing what should be more and what less, one then adds and takes away accordingly. Hence "by this he knows beforehand" — that is, knows by the pattern of turning away and going back. "Turns and transforms" is to turn and change so as to go along with the transformation of things.

The age has no constant nobility; affairs have no constant master. Whoever can be humane is noble, so there is no constant nobility; whoever holds to the good is master, so there is no constant master. The sage constantly gives himself to no one and to everyone; of what he hears there is nothing he does not hear. He must give himself to the good, so there is no one he does not give himself to; words with no warrant he does not listen to, so there is nothing he listens to. ○ Collation: for 無所聽 in the note read 無不聽. He who brings the matter to success and meets the design — with him one makes a master. In affairs he must succeed, in design he must fit; such a man is set up by the common voice and pushed forward to be master. Join with that side and you part from this: design and reckoning cannot be loyal to both. Joining with that side, one must part from this; so his loyal designing cannot be spent on both. There must be a turning and a crossing: turn to this ○ Collation: one copy has 此. and cross to that; cross to this and turn to that. Such is the art. Loyalty cannot be spent on both, so one should practise the art of turning and crossing. "Turning and crossing" is this: meaning to turn and join with this side, one must cross over to that. "Crossing" is setting up matters that look like the truth, so that the blind man does not perceive what one is about.

Use it upon the world, and you must measure the world before giving yourself to it; use it upon a state, and you must measure the state; use it upon a house, and you must measure the house; use it upon a person, and you must measure his talent, capacity, breath and momentum before giving yourself to him. Great or small, advancing or withdrawing, the use of it is one. "Use it" means the art of turning and crossing. "Measure" means to measure whether the enterprise is there or not. "Give oneself to" means to make oneself close. Whoever practises the crossing must praise what their enterprise does contain and so make himself agreeable; then the benighted lord has no way to perceive it, and the art can be practised. Though the art practised differs in great and small, advancing and withdrawing, in praising the enterprise and playing up intimacy it is one. Hence "the use of it is one." One must first settle the design and reckoning, and only then carry it out by the art of flying and clamping. Before practising the art of turning and crossing, one must settle the design; then one carries it out, and uses the art of flying and clamping to stitch up the seams. ○ Collation: the Qian copy lacks 彌; it is supplied from the Daoist Canon copy.

Those of antiquity who were good at turning their backs and turning their faces harmonised the four seas and gathered up the feudal lords, driving them onto the ground of crossing and joining, and there transformed and shifted them; and only then sought the joining. Those of antiquity who knew deeply the pattern of backs and faces made the four seas one and annexed the feudal lords, drove and placed them on the ground of crossing and joining, and then set up methods to change and shift them; the hearts of the many once following, they sought out the true lord and joined with him.

So Yi Yin went five times to Tang and five times to Jie, and could make nothing clear, ○ Collation: the Qian copy lacks 桀; the Daoist Canon copy lacks the six graphs 而不能有所明. and then joined with Tang; and Lü Shang ○ Collation: one copy has 望. went three times to King Wen and three times entered Yin, and could make nothing clear, and then joined with King Wen. That Yi Yin and Lü Shang went to Jie and to Zhou was a crossing, to set them at ease; and these being at ease, they could then join with the true lord. ○ Collation: the Taiping Yulan quotes the chapter "Crossing and Joining": 「伊尹五就桀，五就湯，然後合於湯。呂尚三入殷朝，三就文王，然後合於文王。此天知之至，歸之不疑。」 with the note: 「伊尹、呂尚各以至知說聖王，因擇釣行其術䇿。」 This is to know the clamp of heaven's mandate; hence they gave themselves over without hesitation. Heaven's mandate hung upon Tang of Yin and upon King Wen; hence those two ministers gave themselves to those two lords without hesitation.

Without utmost sagacity reaching into the deeps, one cannot drive an age; without labouring the mind and wearing out thought, one cannot trace a matter to its source; without spending the whole heart to see men's feeling, one cannot make a name; without quickness in the natural stuff, one cannot use troops; being loyal and solid but without truth, one cannot know men. So in the way of crossing and joining, one must first take one's own measure of talent and ability and shrewdness, and weigh who falls short of oneself in long and short, near and far; The way of crossing and joining cannot be worked on one's betters, and must be used on those who are not one's equals; so one learns who is not one's equal, and then practises it. and then one may advance, and may withdraw; may go vertical, and may go horizontal. Practising the way of crossing and joining on those who are not one's equals, advance and withdrawal, vertical and horizontal, are all as one wishes.

7. Estimating揣篇第七

Chuai is to take a thing's weight by hand — to size up the standing of a state and the feeling of a man. It is the great root of all designing, and the rule of all persuasion.

○ Collation: the Taiping Yulan cites it as 《揣情篇》, "Estimating Feeling."

Those of antiquity who were good at using the world must have weighed the world's standing and estimated the feeling of the feudal lords. Weigh their standing inexactly, and you will not know which of them is strong and which weak, which counts heavy and which light. Estimate feeling inexactly, and you will not know the motion and rest of what is hidden and changing.

What is meant by weighing their standing? It is said: gauge them for great and small; reckon them for many and few; count up the sums of goods and money they have and have not; ○ Collation: the Daoist Canon copy has 稱貨財之有無. reckon the people, many or few, plentiful or wanting, and by how much in surplus or in want. Distinguish the shape of the land, hard going or easy: which is to their advantage and which to their harm? In design and deliberation, who is long and who short? In the closeness or estrangement of ruler and minister, who is worthy and who unworthy? And in the wisdom ○ Collation: the Daoist Canon copy has 知𥈠. of their retainers, who has less and who more? Observe the fortune and misfortune of heaven's seasons: which is auspicious and which ill? In the dealings ○ Collation: the Daoist Canon copy has 親; one copy has 親疏. of the lords, who is used and who is not? In the hearts of the hundred families — their turning away and turning to, their shifts and changes — who is safe and who in danger? Who is liked and who hated? Who can be told apart in his tossing and turning? ○ Collation: the Daoist Canon copy has 便 for 辯. He who can know these things is said to weigh their standing. The state of the world must show itself in the weighing. Be good at weighing standing, and the state of it can be known; and for one who knows it and uses it, where can he go and not succeed?

To estimate feeling, one must go to a man at a time of great delight and drive his desire to its utmost: having a desire, he cannot hide his feeling. ○ Collation: the Wenxuan commentary quotes these two sentences with the graphs 藏形 above, which looks to be an error. And one must go to him at a time of great fear and drive his aversion to its utmost: having an aversion, he cannot hide his feeling. Feeling and desire must come out ○ Collation: the Daoist Canon copy has 失 for 出. in that change. It is in men's nature that great delight makes plain what they want and great fear makes plain what they hate; so one takes them where they are plain and drives them to the end. Aversion and desire once driven to the end, the feeling is not hidden. So feeling and desire come out through the changes of delight and fear. ○ Collation: for 生 read 出.

If he is moved and you still do not know the change in him, then set the man aside, do not speak with him, and ask instead of those close to him, and so learn what he is at ease in. If, even taking a time of delight or fear and moving him by desire or aversion, you still do not know the change in him, then set the man aside and do not speak with him; slowly ask instead of those close to him, and his feeling, his desire, and what he rests easy in can be known. When feeling changes within, the form shows without. So one must always know what is hidden through what is seen; and this ○ Collation: one copy lacks 以. is what is called sounding the depths and probing the feeling. Feeling and countenance do not disagree. What changes within must appear without; so one always knows the hidden within from what appears without. He who would watch a man's colour and know his feeling must use this Way; this is what is called sounding the depths and estimating feeling. ○ Collation: for 探情 the Daoist Canon copy has 揣情; on the evidence of the note, 探 looks to be an error.

So in reckoning the affairs of a state one should examine the weighing of standing; in persuading the ruler of men one should examine the estimating of feeling. Design, deliberation, feeling and desire must all come out of this. Examine the weighing of standing and the affairs of a state can be reckoned; examine the estimating of feeling and the ruler of men can be persuaded. As for design, deliberation, feeling and desire, in every case one estimates and then acts. Hence "design, deliberation, feeling and desire must all come out of this." ○ Collation: the Taiping Yulan quotes the chapter "Estimating Feeling": 「說王公君長則審情以說，避所短，從所長。」 Then one may make him honoured or make him base, make him weigh heavy or weigh light, profit him or harm him, bring him to success or to ruin. The count of it is one. Be exact in the art of estimating, and honour and baseness, success and ruin are at one's own disposal, and nothing is not the work of that art. Hence "the count of it is one."

So though one have the Way of the former kings and the designs of sages and wise men, without estimating feeling there is no way to search out what lies hidden. This is the great root of design and the rule of persuasion. The Way of the former kings and the designs of sages and wise men may be vast and broad and deeply subtle; yet without the art of estimating feeling besides, by what should one search out what the other hides? So estimating feeling is truly the great root of design and the rule of persuasion. To be always doing something with men, and that none can get ahead of — to be born ahead of the event: this is the hardest thing of all. He who carries the art of estimating feeling must hold within him the clarity of his own single vision; so in doing something with men, none can get ahead of him. And being able to exhaust the incipient and answer change, he is born ahead of the event. Unless a man embodies the deep and reaches the utmost of the subtle, he cannot do this. Hence "the hardest thing of all." ○ Collation: for 應變 the note in the Daoist Canon copy has 盡變.

So it is said: estimating feeling is the hardest thing to keep watch over, and in speaking one must time one's design to the moment. Human feeling is more perilous than mountains and rivers, and harder to know than heaven. To wish to estimate it and keep watch over it — is that not hard? Hence "estimating feeling is the hardest thing to keep watch over." Design and deliberation come out of human feeling, and one must know the joint of their season: this is why it is hardest of all.

So look at the flitting of gnats and the crawling of worms: there is none without advantage and harm, and from this the beauty of getting a matter under way may be had. Getting a matter under way is the force of the incipient. Flitting gnats and crawling worms are minute creatures, yet they too carry in them a heart for advantage and harm; go with them and they are pleased, cross them and they are roused to fury. How much more a man! How much more ghosts and spirits! So advantage and harm are what the pattern of things cannot be without, and going with and going against are what affairs must run on. To go with brings advantage and to go against brings harm: this is the constant pattern. So looking at this, one may bring the beauty of a matter into being. He who gets a matter under way must examine the force of the incipient and the minute; hence "getting a matter under way is the force of the incipient." So having estimated the feeling, one dresses the words, makes them into a shapely composition, and only then argues the case. Having estimated and known his feeling, one then dresses one's language to lead him; so the persuasion must be made into a shapely composition before the case can be argued.

8. Probing摩篇第八

Mo is to rub, to stroke, to feel a thing over with the hand. Estimating tells you what a man wants; probing is the light touch laid on that want until the answering sign appears in him.

○ Collation: the Taiping Yulan cites it as 《摩息篇》.

Probing is the art of estimating; ○ Collation: the Taiping Yulan quotes the chapter "Probing Intent": 「摩者，揣之也。」 the inner tally is what estimating is master of. Having estimated and known his feeling, one then rubs up against him with what he wants; so probing is the art of estimating. The inner tally is feeling and desire stirring within, with the answering token showing without. The estimator sees the outer token and knows the inner feeling; so the inner tally is what estimating is master of. ○ Collation: the note in the Daoist Canon copy lacks 切. There is a Way to using it, and that Way must be hidden. Estimating is how one gauges what he yearns for; probing is how one stirs his inner tally. He who uses estimating and probing must first settle their pattern; hence "there is a Way to using it." And since one gauges feeling by feeling, and feeling is by nature sunk and secret, "that Way must be hidden."

Subtly ○ Collation: in another copy 㣲 is read with the preceding 隱. probe him with what he wants; sound him and reach in; and by the tally he must answer. Answering, he is bound to act. Having estimated and known which way his feeling leans, one then takes what he wants and rubs him subtly with it; getting what he wants, his feeling must stir. Sound him further and reach in, and the inner tally must answer. The inner tally answering, he is bound to want to do what he is to do. Then withdraw quietly: this is called stopping the cellar-mouth and hiding the end of the thread. Hide your countenance and let your feeling escape, and men will not know; so the matter is accomplished and there is no trouble. The lord meaning to act, the matter is sure to succeed, and one goes along with him. In serving a lord what counts is to have no achievement of one's own and yet a good end; so one withdraws quietly, that is all. If one holds oneself apart from this plan and lets the credit go to the lord, that may be called stopping the cellar-mouth and hiding the end of the thread, hiding one's countenance and letting one's feeling escape. Feeling escaped and the cellar stopped, by what should men come to know? Men not knowing, envy and slander die down; so the matter is accomplished and there is no trouble. ○ Collation: for 僭 in the note read probably 譖.

The probing is here, the answering tally is there; follow it and answer to it, and nothing in the affair is impossible. The probing here is very subtle; the answer there declares itself. The onlooker sees only what declares itself, and not the subtlety. Used so, the credit is wholly the other's; hence "nothing in the affair is impossible." Those of antiquity who were good at probing were like a man who takes his line to the deep pool: he baits it and casts it, and is certain to get his fish. ○ Collation: the Taiping Yulan quotation has 矣 for 焉. Hence it is said: he who governs affairs succeeds day by day and men do not know it; he who governs armies conquers day by day and men do not fear him. The angler shows the bait and hides the hook, so the fish does not see the hook and can be caught; the worthy man shows the achievement and hides the probing, so men do not know the probing and submit of themselves. Hence "he who governs affairs succeeds day by day and men do not know it." Victory in arms comes of good probing; the probing being hidden, there is nothing to fear; hence "he who governs armies conquers day by day and men do not fear him."

The sage designs it in the dark — hence he is called divine; brings it to pass in the light — hence he is called clear. Designing in the hidden and the secret, used daily and never known, like the unfathomable Way of the spirits — hence "divine." Merit accomplished and the affair brought through, blazing plain for all — hence "clear." What is meant by "he who governs affairs succeeds day by day" is the heaping up of virtue. The people are put at ease by his use of them and do not know what does them good: this is the heaping up of goodness. ○ Collation: the Qian copy has 智 for 也. The people take his Way and do not know why it is so; and the world likens him to the divine and the clear. The sage embodies the Way and sets up a teaching, stands third with heaven and earth and spreads their transforming, sheathes his brightness and covers his tracks, hides his working and shows his humaneness. So the people rest in his virtue and do not know what does them good, follow his Way and do not know why it is so; hence they liken him to the divine and the clear. "He who governs armies conquers day by day" fights always without contending and without expense, and the people do not know why they submit nor why they fear; and the world likens him to the divine and the clear. The good fighter cuts off calamity within his own breast and forbids evil before it sprouts; so he makes not contending his warfare. The host does not take the field, so the state's revenue is not spent. His utmost virtue runs hidden and free and his deep wind fans afar; merit accomplished and the affair brought through, the hundred families all get what is natural to them. So they do not know why they submit nor why they fear, and liken him to the divine and the clear.

In ○ Collation: another copy lacks 其. probing there is the level and the upright; the delighted and the angry; the name and the deed; the clean and the trustworthy; the profitable and the lowly. All ten of these are what probing sets out from. Men's stuff and nature are uneven and the business of affairs shifts; so the prober too waxes and wanes, fills and empties, and moves as the incipient moment gives him leave. The level is stillness; the upright is fitness; ○ Collation: the Daoist Canon copy has 直. the delighted is pleasure; the angry is motion; the name is what is sent forth; the deed is what is brought to completion; the clean is purity; the trustworthy is keeping to the appointed time; ○ Collation: the Daoist Canon copy has 明. the profitable is seeking; the lowly is flattery. In a name what counts is that it be sent forth and raised high, hence "what is sent forth." In a deed what counts is that it succeed, hence "what is brought to completion." So what the sage alone uses, the common run of men all have; yet they win no success, because they use it wrongly. The ten matters above the sage alone uses for probing, and can thereby succeed and get affairs established; yet there is no one who does not have them. It is because they use them not in the right Way that they cannot succeed.

So of designs, none is harder than to be thorough and secret; of persuasions, none is harder than to be heard out entire; of affairs, none is harder than to be sure of success. ○ Collation: for these two clauses the Taiping Yulan quotation has 悉行 for 悉聴, and quotes the note: 「摩不失其情，故能建功。」 These three only the sage can take ○ Collation: the Daoist Canon copy lacks 任. upon himself. A design not thorough and secret loses the moment and its accomplishment is spoiled; a persuasion not heard out entire goes against the pattern and breeds doubt; an affair not sure of success stops at the last basketful and is abandoned. All are hard; who can take them on and be without doubt but the sage? So a design must be thorough and secret, and one must choose someone open to it to speak to. Hence it is said: some knots have no gap in them. Lay a design before one open to it, and he receives it empty, as a stone dropped into water is received, as an opened channel takes the spring; and then what gap is to be found? Hence "a knot with no gap." ○ Collation: for 受 in the note another copy has 更. An affair succeeds only by fitting the numbers; hence it is said: the Way, the numbers and the season go paired. A design succeeds only if one has first checked it against the numbers of the art; so the Way, the art and the season, these three, must go paired and matched, and then the affair can succeed and the work be established. A persuasion is heard only by fitting the feeling; hence it is said: what fits the feeling gets heard. What can make a persuasion be listened to, if not its fitting the feeling?

So things go home to their kind: carry faggots toward a fire and the dry catch first; pour water on level ground and the wet parts soak first. This is how things of a kind answer ○ Collation: the Yilin quotes 「此類相應也」. one another under a force, and the likeness holds. This says that the answer of the inner tally to the outer probing is just so. It says that the inner tally answers when the outer probing hits the kind, just as water runs to the wet and fire travels to the dry. So it is said: probe a man by his kind and there is that in him which does not answer; probe him by his desire and there is that in him which will not listen. Hence it is called the Way that walks alone. Who is good at probing but the sage? Hence "the Way that walks alone." He who has the incipient is not late in succeeding, and is not held back; he takes his time and the transformation is accomplished. See the incipient and act — where is the lateness? Succeed and do not sit on the credit — where is the long detention? Do these two and one may transform the world.

9. Weighing Words權篇第九

A catalogue of speech: what persuasion is for, the five counterfeit kinds of talk, the five disorders of the voice, and the rule for pitching one's words to the man in front of one.

○ Collation: the Taiping Yulan cites it as 《量權篇》, "Measuring the Weight."

To persuade is to talk a man round; to talk him round is to take something from him. "To persuade" is to persuade the other man; "to talk him round" is to have something to take from him. Zi is "to take." To dress one's words is to borrow; and borrowing means adding and taking away. The persuader ornaments his language, borrowing only so as to get in with the other; it is not the heart of the business. And having borrowed, he must add and take away; hence "borrowing means adding and taking away." Reply and rejoinder are nimble speech; and nimble speech is light argument. When the other asks something and one answers on the instant, it is only nimble speech. Where the aim of the wording is nimbleness, what one argues is naturally nimble speech and not the deepest word. To make the point good is to make it clear; and making it clear is verifying by the tally. Checking the facts of a matter so as to make the point good is meant to make plain what is true and what false. True and false once plain, the tally's verification declares itself; hence "making it clear is verifying by the tally." Speech may turn and turn back, each side wishing to put the other off. To object is to argue back; and arguing back is angling for the incipient. Where speech does not agree, they turn and turn and press each other, and so argue back against what was said before. He who argues back must have the pattern fine and the matter clear, and then the incipient and minute can be got at and exhausted. Hence "arguing back is angling for the incipient." To seek out what is deep and hidden is called angling. ○ Collation: the Qian copy lacks the eight graphs 言或反覆，欲相却也; the Daoist Canon copy has them, and they must be part of the text — as the note shows.

Fawning speech flatters and angles for a name for loyalty. The flatterer anticipates the wish and falls in with the desire, and so seeks a name for loyalty; hence "flatters and angles for a name for loyalty." Toadying speech is copious and angles for a name for wit. The copious man heaps up citations and ornaments of phrase, and so seeks a name for wit; hence "is copious and angles for a name for wit." Level speech is decisive and angles for a name for courage. The decisive man lets go and lets be without hesitation, and so seeks a name for courage; hence "is decisive and angles for a name for courage." Grieving speech is calculating and angles for a name for good faith. Qi is "sorrow": one puts on a show of grief and sets out one's words. The calculating man picks his plans and puts them forward, and so seeks a name for good faith; hence "is calculating and angles for a name for good faith." Quiet speech turns things back and angles for a name for winning. Quiet speech means putting on a show of calm purity and setting out one's words. "Turning back" means first laying out another's shortcomings so as to stop up his fault, and so seeking a name for winning; hence "turns things back and angles for a name for winning."

Anticipating the wish and completing the desire is flattery. Heaping up citations and ornaments of phrase is copiousness. Letting go and letting be unfittingly is decisiveness. Picking plans and putting them forward is calculation. Laying out another's ○ Collation: for 他 the Daoist Canon copy has 先, and so in the note. shortcomings and stopping up his fault is turning back. A man who is in fact deficient and does not know it and reproach himself within, but attacks another's faults instead and stops the other up as being in the wrong — such a man is "turning back."

So the mouth is a trigger and a gate, the means of barring feeling and intent. Eyes and ears are the heart's helpers, the means of spying out wickedness. Hence it is said: let the three be tuned together and answer one another, and move along the advantageous way. The mouth is what sends out speech; hence "a trigger and a gate." Whether feeling and intent are let out lies with that trigger; hence "the means of opening and barring feeling and intent." Eyes and ears help the heart to reach the pattern of things; hence "the heart's helpers." With eyes and ears the heart can spy the chinks and see the other's wickedness; hence "spying out wickedness." Let ears, eyes and heart, these three, be tuned and answer one another, and every move succeeds and nothing is unlucky; and the reason nothing is unlucky is that they move along the Way. Hence "let the three be tuned together and answer one another, and move along the advantageous way." ○ Collation: for 開閉 in the note the Daoist Canon copy has 闗閉. So to speak copiously without confusion, to wheel and soar without losing the way, to shift and alter without danger, comes of seeing the essential and getting the pattern. Once one can see the essential and get the pattern, one can complete things by indirection without loss. So one may speak copiously, in a tangle of blossom, and not be confused; wheel and soar beyond the road and not lose the way; shift and alter the ordinary and not be in danger.

So the eyeless cannot be shown the five colours; the earless cannot be told the five notes. The five colours are displayed for those who have eyes, so the eyeless cannot be shown them; the five notes are made for those who have ears, so the earless cannot be told them. Shallow cramp and dark clogging are constantly shut and do not get through; hence the sage does not busy himself with them.

The ancients had a saying: the mouth may be used to eat, but not to speak. Speech has things it must avoid; the mouths of the many will melt metal, because speech has its crooked reasons. What the mouth eats can fatten the whole body, so it may be used to eat; what the mouth says may call down a hundred calamities, so it may not be used to speak. A speaker touches on what must be avoided; hence "has things it must avoid." Metal is a hard thing, and the mouths of the many can melt it — because the mouths of the many have their private crookedness; hence "speech has its crooked reasons."

It is the way of men that when they speak they want to be listened to, and when they undertake a thing they want it to succeed. Being listened to lies in fitting the other; succeeding lies in going with the pattern. This opens what follows. Therefore the wise man does not use what he himself is short in, but uses what the foolish man is long in; does not use what he himself is clumsy at, but uses what the foolish man is skilled at; and so he is never at a loss. The wise man's short cannot beat the fool's long; the wise man's clumsiness cannot beat the fool's skill. So it is said: there are five disorders of speech — sickness, resentment, worry, anger and delight. Let any one of the five be present and the speech loses its balance and does not run clear. The humane man holds goods light and cannot be lured by profit, but can be got to spend; the brave man holds danger light and cannot be frightened by peril, but can be got to hold a dangerous post; the wise man reaches through the numbers and is clear in the pattern, and cannot be cheated by what is untrue, but can be shown the pattern of the Way and got to establish merit. So the strong is built up out of weakness, the straight out of crookedness, abundance out of want. This is the art of the Way at work. Having little, one may gain the many; so want heaped up makes abundance. To make strength of weakness, straightness of crookedness, abundance of want — this is where the art of the Way runs; hence "the art of the Way at work."

So with him who is close without and distant within, speak to the within; with him who is close within and distant without, speak to the without. Whose person is within and whose words go outside is estranged; whose person is outside and whose words cut deep is in danger. Do not force on a man what he does not want; do not teach a man by what he does not know. 11. Deciding決篇第十一

Every decision rests on a doubt. Men doubt because they cannot see where the good lies and where the harm; the art of deciding is to draw that out and then cut.

In deciding anything, one must rest on the man's doubt. He wishes well to what brings him blessing and ill to what brings him trouble; and the harm of it comes down to the drawing-out. There is doubt, and then decision; hence "must rest on the man's doubt." It is in men's nature to wish well to what brings blessing and ill to what brings trouble. Doubt is born where the pattern of blessing and trouble is not yet clear; hence "he wishes well to what brings him blessing and ill to what brings him trouble." But he who is good at deciding a doubt must draw out the man's feeling, and only then can cut for or against. Be in the end without confusion and without partiality, and there is advantage in it; take the advantage away and he will not accept, and the thing is given over to the unexpected. To harbour doubt is confusion; to be not upright is partiality. If the decider can be without confusion and partiality, the doer can get through; and blessing and advantage are born of it. But take the blessing and advantage away, and the doubter will not change his mind at the decision, and one must rather have him give his intent over to the unexpected. To run to the odd and alter the constant is called the unexpected. ○ Collation: for 更 in the note the Daoist Canon copy has 受; the thirteen graphs from 更使 onward are missing in the Daoist Canon copy. If a thing is to his advantage and good, and you covertly put it to him as an evil, he will not accept it, and it makes for estrangement and distance. The doubter is set on his advantage and his good; if the decider hides the advantage and the good and puts the matter to him as an evil, he does not accept the decision, and it makes for estrangement instead. So there are ways of making a man lose his advantage, and ways of making him fall away into harm: these are failures in the handling of a matter. In both the above cases — taking away the advantage, and putting it as an evil — the doubter does not change at the decision, and what he then does cannot get through; hence the ruin of lost advantage and of falling into harm. All these are failures in deciding a matter.

The sage has five means of bringing his affairs to success: there is deciding by open virtue; deciding by hidden attack; deciding by good faith and sincerity; deciding by covering and concealing; deciding by the plain and ordinary. The sage is good at change and passage and sounds the pattern of things, and in every matter he decides he looks to certain success. Where the matter is done and the pattern plain, he decides by open virtue; where the feeling is hidden and the words feigned, by hidden attack; where the Way is sincere and the will straight, by good faith and sincerity; where the wickedness is small and the harm slight, by covering and concealing; where a man follows the constant and keeps to the old, by the plain and ordinary. Yang strives after the one, yin after the two; the plain and ordinary and the trigger of the pivot: use these four, and apply them subtly. Li is "to strive." Yang is the ruler's way, so what it says must strive after the one; the one is doing nothing. Yin is the minister's way, so what it says must strive after the two; the two is doing something. The ruler's way does nothing, so it takes the plain and ordinary for its master; the minister's way does something, so it takes the trigger of the pivot for its use. That is, the one, the two, the plain and ordinary, the trigger of the pivot: in whatever these four are applied to, the application must be fine and subtle and match the marvellous, and then the matter goes forward and the pattern is not blocked.

Then gauge it against what is past, verify it against what is coming, check it against the plain and ordinary; and if it can be done, decide it. Ruler and minister having their settled parts, one then gauges the past and verifies the coming, checks against the plain and ordinary, and reckons right and wrong; and the pattern permitting, decides it.

In the affairs of kings, dukes and great men: what is precarious but carries a fine name — if it can be done, decide it. Wei, "precarious," is here "high." Where the matter is high and the name fine, decide it. What costs no strength and is easily accomplished — if it can be done, decide it. What is called "generous without expense"; hence decide it. What costs strength and bitter labour, but cannot be helped and must be done — if it can be done, decide it. What is called "knowing that nothing can be done about it and resting in it as in fate"; hence decide it. What rids him of trouble — if it can be done, decide it. What follows his blessing — if it can be done, decide it. To rid a man of trouble and follow his blessing is the great going-with of the pattern; hence decide it.

So deciding feeling and settling doubt is the foundation ○ Collation: for 基 the Daoist Canon copy has 機. of ten thousand affairs: by it order and disorder are set right and success and failure decided, and it is hard to do. Order and disorder are set right by it and success and failure decided by it; miss by a hair and you are a thousand li out. It is the loosing of the trigger, the master of honour and disgrace; hence "hard to do." So the former kings used milfoil and tortoise-shell, to decide for themselves. If the sagely wit of the former kings, which nothing escaped, still used milfoil and shell to decide by, how much less may those beneath them trust wholly to themselves and not seek design widely among those who understand!

12. Words as Tallies符言第十二

Nine short articles for a ruler, each closed by its rubric — "the above concerns the seat," "the above concerns clear sight," and so on. Words that answer the event as the two halves of a tally answer one another.

Words sent out that are always borne out, as the halves of a tally fit; hence "words as tallies."

Be at ease, unhurried, upright and still, and what your regulation reaches will be fat. Bei is "to reach"; rou, "fat," is here abundance. If a man in office can be at ease, unhurried, upright and still, then there is no rule of his that does not reach abundance. ○ Collation: for 先肉 the Daoist Canon copy has 無不肉. The above ○ Collation: for 右 the Daoist Canon copy has 有, and so throughout below. concerns the ruler's seat. What governs the seat is being at ease, unhurried, upright and still — no more.

In the eye, clear sight is prized; in the ear, keen hearing; in the heart, wit. The eye being clear, nothing looked at is unseen; the ear keen, nothing listened to is unheard; the heart witted, no thought fails to get through. Let these three be unobstructed, and what could one set one's hand to that would not be right? See with the eyes of the world and there is nothing unseen; hear with the ears of the world and there is nothing unheard; think ○ Collation: for 心 the Dengxizi has 智; the Daoist Canon copy lacks 思. and deliberate with the heart of the world and there is nothing unknown. When they converge like spokes and come forward together, the clarity cannot be stopped up. The sage does not use his own keenness, clarity and thought, but gives the office over to the world; so the clear-sighted see for him, the keen-eared hear for him, the witted design for him — as clouds follow the dragon and wind follows the tiger, a flood that nothing stems. Converging like spokes and coming forward together, is it not fitting that they should shine like the sun and moon? How could that be stopped up? Hence "the clarity cannot be stopped up."

The above concerns clear sight. What governs clear sight is seeing with the eyes of the world.

The art of virtue says: do not stiffen and refuse. The art of raising virtue lies in being wide and taking much in. The mountain does not turn away dust, and so can reach its height; the sea does not refuse the streams, and so can reach its depth; the sage does not refuse the many, and so can reach his greatness. Hence "do not stiffen and refuse." Grant them and they guard you; refuse them and you are shut in. Grant and take men in, and they must come over and guard you; refuse and go against them, and they must turn away and shut you in. Coming over and guarding, danger can be made safe; turning away and shutting, what was open is blocked. He who would raise his virtue — how can he not take widely in? Look up at the high mountain and its top can be reached; sound the deep pool and its bottom can be touched. But the virtue and art of the divine and clear, upright and still — nothing reaches its end. Nothing is higher than a mountain, and still it can be topped; nothing deeper than a pool, and still it can be sounded. But in the seat of the divine and clear, with the virtue and art upright and still, go to meet it and you do not see its front, follow it and you do not see its back — can it be measured?

The above concerns virtue. What governs virtue lies in holding widely and not refusing.

In reward, good faith is prized; in punishment, uprightness. Reward being faithful, the man who has won merit will give his life and throw it away for you; punishment being upright, the man who is executed will go to his death without resentment. ○ Collation: for 更 the Daoist Canon copy has 受. In rewards and gifts good faith is prized: they must be verified by what ear and eye have heard and seen; and then what is not heard and seen will be silently transformed as well. Where kindness is shown and reward given on what ear and eye have heard and seen, one can verify without error, and every act answers the merit; and so good faith goes before the word, and even what is not heard and seen is silently transformed. Sincerity floods the world and the spirits — how much more will it reach the wicked man who would usurp his lord! If reward always follows good faith, the utmost sincerity floods the world and the spirits guard him; as an infant has heaven's bounty, so he does not topple, being firm as Mount Tai. How much more the disaffected who would loose their wicked designs and usurp the lord's seat! They are rotten flesh meeting the edge of a keen sword: there will be no trouble.

The above concerns reward. What governs reward is prizing good faith.

The first is heaven's; the second, earth's; the third, man's. Heaven has its reckoning of the contrary and the compliant; earth has its pattern of the void and the solitary; man has his division of the open and the blocked. He who holds the world should know them all. Of the four quarters and above and below, of left and right and front and back — where does the baleful planet stand? The four quarters, above and below, left and right, front and back all have their fitness of yin and yang, of facing and turning away; he who takes affairs in hand in a state must know it. Moreover Mars is the punishing star of heaven, and where it lodges calamity falls and fortune and misfortune are especially marked. Hence it is said: though there be an enlightened Son of Heaven, he must still find where Mars stands. So this too must be known.

The above concerns inquiry. What governs inquiry requires distinguishing the Way of the three powers.

The heart governs the nine apertures; the ruler is chief of the five offices. What the nine apertures do is at the heart's bidding; what the five offices do is at the ruler's command. He who does good, the ruler gives reward; he who does wrong, the ruler gives punishment. To reward the good and punish the wrong is the great constant of government. If the ruler, taking what men seek, gives them accordingly, he is not wearied. The giver answers what the other seeks; the seeker, being answered, is pleased. Answer the seeking, and taking and giving are not idly done; being answered, men go about it without slackening. Move with men's nature — where is the weariness in that? The sage uses this, and so can reward them; going by it and following the pattern, he can indeed ○ Collation: one copy has 故; the Dengxizi also has 故. 故 and 固 were interchangeable in old writing. last long. To give in answer to seeking — no pleasure is greater. Though there be no jade or silk in it, the encouragement is as good as a reward. But to go against the pattern — no calamity is swifter. Going by it and following the pattern, he can last long.

The above concerns going by things. What governs going by things prizes following the pattern.

The ruler of men must be thorough; if he is not thorough, disorder springs up among his ministers. "Thorough" is knowing the pattern of things all round; not being thorough in the pattern, his ministers fall into disorder. Their business becomes inconstant; inner and outer do not communicate, and how should he know where anything opens? Jia is as much as "enterprise." The ministers being in disorder, what they are at has no constancy; inner and outer are shut, and every road is blocked — how should he know where anything opens? Opening and shutting being ill done, he does not see the source. Opening and shutting is the baihe of the first chapter. Not using the pattern of opening and closing, he does not see the source of doing well.

The above concerns thoroughness. What governs thoroughness lies in knowing the pattern of things all round.

The first is the far-reaching eye; the second, the flying ear; the third, the planted clarity. Seeing with the eyes of the world, hence "the far-reaching eye"; hearing with the ears of the world, hence "the flying ear"; thinking with the heart of the world, hence "the planted clarity." To know clearly what is beyond a thousand li and what lies hidden and minute — this is called seeing through the world, and no wickedness is not silently altered. Think with the heart of the world and nothing is unknown. So beyond a thousand li, and in the hidden and minute, there is nothing not deeply surveyed. The hidden and minute being examined, the wicked cut off their crookedness within their own breasts; hence "no wickedness is not silently altered." Geng is "to change." ○ Collation: the Daoist Canon copy has 更 after 闇變 in the text, which agrees with the note.

The above concerns reverent attention. What governs it lies in being keen, clear, cultivated and thoughtful.

Act according to the name, and the substance is secure and whole. The substance answering the name is what makes it secure and whole. Name and substance give birth to one another, and turning back make each other's reality. Act according to the name and you get the substance; from the substance the name is born. Name and substance not falling short, the reality is there within them. So it is said: the name, when apt, is born of the substance, and the substance is born of the pattern. The apt name is born of itself out of the substance; the substance once standing, the pattern is born of itself. The pattern is born of the virtue of name and substance; Where no pattern is inapt, the virtue of name and substance is born of itself. virtue is born of harmony, and harmony of aptness. Have virtue and there must be harmony; be able to harmonise and you are apt of yourself.

The above concerns the name. What governs the name lies in matching the substance.

The Seven Arts of the Yin Tally: the Root Canon夲經隂符七術

The first twelve chapters look outward, at other men. These seven look inward, at the stock of spirit, will, intent and momentum that any of it must be spent from. Each art is set under the figure of a creature: the five dragons, the sacred tortoise, the teng-serpent, the crouching bear, the bird of prey, the fierce beast, the sacred milfoil.

The yin tally: the will is kept private within and things answer without, as two halves of a tally fit; hence "yin tally." It runs from the root through to the branches; hence "root canon."

Filling the Spirit, on the model of the Five Dragons盛神法五龍

Filling the spirit, on the model of the five dragons. The five dragons are the dragons of the five phases. The dragon's transformations are without end and the spirit is unfathomable as yin and yang; so the Way of filling the spirit takes the five dragons for its model. In the full spirit are five breaths; spirit is their chief, the heart their lodging, virtue their greatness; and the nourishing of spirit finds its home in the Way. The five breaths are the breaths of the five viscera: essence, spirit, hun soul, po soul and will. Spirit dwells in the midst of the other four, hence "their chief"; the heart can hold and contain, hence "their lodging"; virtue can govern and drive, hence "their greatness." So what is fit for nourishing the spirit finds its home in the Way.

The Way is the beginning of heaven and earth, and the One is its thread. It is what things are made from and what heaven gives birth to; it wraps all round and has no form; the breath it transforms was complete before heaven and earth. None sees its shape, none knows its name: it is called the divine numen. The nameless is the beginning of heaven and earth, hence "the Way is the beginning of heaven and earth." What the Way first gives birth to is the One, hence "the One is its thread." The Way of heaven is a blended whole; yin and yang are its potter's wheel, by which the ten thousand things are made and transformed and heaven and earth given birth and completion. It holds all in a vast embrace, and none sees its shape; and as for the breath of its transforming, that was complete before heaven and earth, and cannot be pinned down by look or figure nor traced by name. It is spoken of as that which makes the ten thousand things marvellous; hence "called the divine numen."

So the Way is the source of the divine and clear, and the One is the start of its transforming. Therefore virtue nourishes the five breaths, and when the heart can get the One, then it has its art. The divine and clear receive the Way and so are born, hence "the Way is the source of the divine and clear." If the start of transforming were not one, there would be times when it did not transform, hence "the One is the start of its transforming." To follow the pattern and so succeed is called virtue; if the five breaths can each follow its pattern, success can be had — hence "virtue nourishes the five breaths." The One is what does nothing and is so of itself. Let the heart do nothing and its art is born of itself; hence "when the heart can get the One, then it has its art." The art is the Way of the heart's breath. Where it lodges, there the spirit is set to work. The heart's breath matching the Way of what is so of itself, art can then be born; and the art having a Way to lodge in, the spirit is then set to work.

The nine apertures and the twelve lodgings are the gates of the breath and the heart's general holding. He who receives his life from heaven is called a true man; the true man is one with heaven. The twelve lodgings: the eye sees colour, the ear hears sound, the nose smells scent, the mouth knows flavour, the body feels touch, the mind thinks of matters — faculty and object lodging by turns in one another. The lodgings are twelve, hence "the twelve lodgings." The breath goes in and out by them, hence "the gates of the breath." It is only as the heart takes up or lets go, hence "the heart's general holding." All this is received from heaven; and not to diminish that original stock is to be a "true man." The true man is of one body with heaven, hence "one with heaven." He who knows it by inward cultivation and practice is called a sage; the sage knows it by kinds. "Inward cultivation and practice" means knowing by borrowed learning. Sage though he is, the sage still knows by borrowed learning; and borrowed learning is not the natural — hence "knows it by kinds."

So men and life are one, and come out of the transformation of things. Men live together between heaven and earth, and what they get is one and the same; but once they have come forth, they are transformed along with things, and so are not alike. Knowing kinds lies in the apertures; where there is something doubted, it must pass through the heart's art; and where the heart has not the art, something must fail to get through. The apertures are the nine apertures. Knowing the kinds of things lies in the nine apertures, but what the apertures doubt must be in communication with the heart's art; and if the heart has not the art, the art must fail to get through. When it does get through, the five breaths are nourished, and the business is to lodge the spirit: this is what is called transformation. The heart's art getting through, the five breaths nourish themselves; and in nourishing the five breaths the business is to have the spirit come home and lodge. The spirit once lodged, one is transformed along the pattern of itself.

In the transformation are five breaths: the will, the thought, the spirit, the virtue; and the spirit is chief of them. He who is still and harmonious nourishes his breath, ○ Collation: the Daoist Canon copy has 養. and the breath gets its harmony. Let the four not decay, and the awe and force at the four borders will all be there to be kept and lodged: this is called spirit-transformation; and when it comes home to the person, that is the true man. To be able to transform lies in keeping the five breaths whole. "The spirit is chief" means being able to make will and thought one and rule over them. The spirit being chief and one, one can be still and harmonious and nourish the breath; the breath nourished, virtue must be harmonious. The four are will, thought, spirit and virtue. If these four do not decay, then the awe and force at the four borders are all there for constant keeping and lodging; and the transformations of the spirit-Way come home to the person of themselves. Spirit-transformation coming home to the person, one may be called a true man.

The true man is one with heaven and joined to the Way; he holds the One and nourishes and brings forth the ten thousand kinds; he carries heaven's heart in him, dispenses virtue and nurture, and does nothing, and so wraps up will and deliberation, thought and intent, and puts forth awe and force. The gentleman reaches through to this; the spirit being full, he can then nourish the will. The One is doing nothing. The true man nourishes and brings forth the ten thousand kinds, carries heaven's heart in his breast, dispenses virtue and rears them, and does all of it by doing nothing; hence "holds the One and nourishes and brings forth the ten thousand kinds." As for will and intent, thought and deliberation, and the working of awe and force, none of it is other than natural, moving along the pattern; hence "does nothing, and so wraps up." And who reaches through to this Way but the man who is good at being a gentleman? The spirit being made full, one may then nourish the will.

Nourishing the Will, on the model of the Sacred Tortoise養志法靈龜

Nourishing the will, on the model of the sacred tortoise. The will examines right and wrong; the tortoise knows fortune and misfortune; hence "nourishing the will, on the model of the sacred tortoise." Nourishing the will is for when the thought of the heart's breath does not get through. Because the heart's breath does not get through, one must nourish the will to seek a passage. Where there is something the will wants, one keeps it and thinks on it. The will is the servant of desire. Desire being much, the heart is scattered; the heart scattered, the will decays; the will decayed, thought does not get through. This shows that the man who gives rein to desire cannot nourish his breath and will, so that what he thinks does not get through. So when the heart's breath is one, desire does not waver; desire not wavering, will and intent do not decay; will and intent not decaying, thought and pattern get through. This shows that the man of few desires can nourish his will, so that thought and pattern get through. Pattern getting through, there is harmony and passage; and with harmony and passage, no disordered breath troubles the breast. Harmony and passage leave nothing untuned, so disordered breath goes of itself.

So within one nourishes the will, and without one knows men. Nourish the will and the heart gets through; know men and the division of offices is clear. The heart getting through, the whole body is at peace; office being clear, the world is at peace. If you would use this upon a man, you must first know how he nourishes his breath and will — know whether his breath is full or spent, nourish his breath and will, examine what he is at ease in, and so know what he is able to do. "Use it upon a man" means using men by the art of nourishing the will. Nourish the will and the breath is full; do not, and it is spent. Full or spent once shaped, what he is at ease in and able to do can be known. And who is good at nourishing the will but the man of few desires?

Where the will is not nourished, the heart's breath is not firm; the heart's breath not firm, thought and deliberation do not get through; thought and deliberation not getting through, will and intent are not solid; will and intent not solid, reply and rejoinder have no force; reply and rejoinder having no force, the will is lost and the heart's breath is empty; the will lost and the heart's breath empty, the spirit is gone. This shows that the losing of the spirit begins from not nourishing the will. The spirit gone, one is a blur; being a blur, the three do not meet as one. "A blur" describes what is not sharp and clear. "The three meeting" means the meeting of will, heart and spirit. The spirit not being sharp and clear, there is much that goes wrong and astray, so the three that meet do not meet as one.

At the start of nourishing the will the business is to put oneself at ease. Oneself at ease, will and intent are solid and firm; will and intent solid and firm, the awe and force are not scattered, the divine clarity comes and keeps constant guard, and one can then scatter another's. "At ease" means the heart at ease through few desires. The awe and force not being scattered, the divine clarity comes constantly to guard; and so awe is heaped up and force shakes things. The first "scatter" means dispersed and lost; the second means that I have the awe and can move the other — hence "one can then scatter another's."

Solidifying the Intent, on the model of the Teng-Serpent實意法螣蛇

Solidifying the intent, on the model of the teng-serpent. The intent has its windings and the serpent can coil and stretch; so solidifying the intent takes the teng-serpent for its model. Solidifying the intent is the deliberating of the breath. The intent being solid, the breath is level; the breath level, the deliberation is exact. Hence "solidifying the intent is the deliberating of the breath." The heart should be at rest and still, the deliberation deep and far-reaching. The heart at rest and still, the spirit's plans are born; ○ Collation: for 神䇿生 the Daoist Canon copy has 神明榮. the deliberation deep and far-reaching, the design is complete. The spirit's plans born, the will cannot be thrown into disorder; the design complete, the work cannot be broken into. Wit cannot be disordered, so the design can be completed; the work cannot be broken into, so the realm can be made quiet. Intent and deliberation settled, the heart is thereupon at ease, what one does goes not amiss, and the spirit is got of itself; and being got, it sets. The heart at ease, one does nothing and follows the pattern, does not think and yet deeply surveys; so what the heart does goes not amiss and the spirit is got of itself, and being got, nothing fails to be accomplished. To "set" is to be accomplished.

When the recognising breath is only a lodger, wickedness leans upon it and false designs ○ Collation: the Daoist Canon copy has 得 after both 邪 and 謀. confuse it, and the words no longer come from the heart. "Lodger" means a guest lodging; that is, the recognising breath is not the true one but only a guest. So wickedness gets its chance to lean on it and false design to confuse it; and then all the words come from the breast and no longer from the heart. So trust the heart's art, keep the true One and be not transformed, and wait for the meeting of another's intent and deliberation: this is the moment for listening. Let the heart's art be sincere and clear and not fall short, and the true One be kept fast and not transformed; and then in dealing with men and meeting things, the other must pour out his sincerity and spend his whole intent. The witted deliberate for him and the clear-sighted offer plans; above and below being of one heart, design and deliberation can meet. Hearing with the ears of the world, the signs of things can be known.

Design and reckoning are the trigger of survival and ruin; if the deliberation does not meet, the listening is not exact. The moment missed, the design is lost, and then the intent has nothing to trust to, and is empty and without substance. Get the reckoning and you survive; lose it and you perish. Hence "design and reckoning are the trigger of survival and ruin." If the deliberation does not meet things, the hearer does not hear for you, so the listening is not exact. The listening not exact, how should the moment be caught? Design against the moment — what is that but loss? The reckoning lost, what has the intent to trust to? There is only emptiness and falseness, and no sincerity left. ○ Collation: the seventeen graphs from 計旣失矣 in the note are missing in the Daoist Canon copy. So the deliberation of design and reckoning has its business in solidifying the intent; and solidifying the intent must begin from the heart's art. Solidify the intent and the design is got, hence "its business is in solidifying the intent." Solid intent comes of the heart being at ease, hence "must begin from the heart's art." ○ Collation: the Daoist Canon copy wrongly takes this passage of text for commentary, and also lacks the eighteen graphs of the note from 實意 onward.

Doing nothing, seek to put the five viscera at rest and make the six receptacles harmonious and open, so that essence, spirit and the two souls keep firm guard and do not stir; then one can look inward, listen backward and settle the will. Deliberate in the great void and wait for the spirit's coming and going. To seek the Way of a heart at ease one must first be quiet, plain and doing nothing; then the five viscera are at rest, the six receptacles open and harmonious, essence, spirit and the two souls each keep their charge and, plain and quiet, do not stir. Then one may look inward upon the formless, listen backward upon the soundless, house will and deliberation in the great void, and have the divine clarity come and go a thousand thousand times and come home to oneself. ○ Collation: for 宅 the Daoist Canon copy has 定.

So one observes the opening out of heaven and earth, knows how the ten thousand things are made and transformed, sees the end and beginning of yin and yang, traces to its source the ordering of human affairs; without going out of doors one knows the world, without looking through the window one sees the Way of heaven; without seeing, one names; without travelling, one arrives. Only the spirit is still and does not move, and being stirred, reaches through to every cause under heaven; so it can know by not knowing and see by not seeing. Why should it wait to go out of doors or look through the window before knowing and seeing? So too with "without seeing, one names; without travelling, one arrives." This is called knowing by the Way; by it one reaches through to the divine clarity, answers where there is no quarter, and the spirit lodges in one. The Way does not think and does not act. Then does he who knows by the Way know by using knowledge? It is by his not knowing that he can reach through to the divine clarity, answer where there is no quarter, and have the spirit come and lodge. Su is as much as "to lodge."

Dividing the Awe, on the model of the Crouching Bear分威法伏熊

Dividing the awe, on the model of the crouching bear. Essence emptied and things moved is called awe; loosed near and shaking far is called dividing. The bear, when it strikes, must first crouch and then move; hence "dividing the awe, on the model of the crouching bear." Dividing the awe is the spirit's covering. Fu, "covering," is as much as clothing. The divine clarity being one's clothing, the awe may then be divided. So still the intent and make the will firm, and the spirit comes home to its lodging, and the awe's covering is ample. The Way of bringing the spirit in requires stilling the intent and making the will firm; it then comes home to its lodging of itself, and the spirit's covering of awe grows ample. The lodging is the dwelling of will and intent. The awe's covering ample, the inside is solid and firm; the inside solid and firm, none can withstand it; none withstanding it, one can divide off other men's awe and move their force as heaven moves. The outward awe being ample, the inward will is firm and solid; outside and inside answering each other, who dares withstand it? Nothing being able to withstand it, my awe is divided off. The awe divided and the force moving, all things grow grave and fear and reverence the man as they do heaven.

By the solid take the empty; by the having take the not-having, as one weighs grains against an ingot. The awe and force being ample and men and things grave, I have substance and they are empty and have none; so I can take their emptiness with my solidity and their not-having with my having. And in the taking, every motion answers, as one weighs grains to make up an ingot. Twenty-four grains make an ounce, twenty-four ounces an ingot. So whoever moves is followed, whoever calls is answered; crook one finger and watch what follows in order; motion and change take visible form and none can break in. The awe divided and the force shaking, all things bend like grass in wind; so every motion has its following and every call its answer. Only crook a finger and call them by name, and the whole company comes. Then slowly, in order, watch the rest; the many follow their nature and rest in it, and each is given his place. So one stirs them as a wind does and changes them as a transformation does, and as clay on the wheel takes the shapes of the whole array of vessels of itself, so the world gladly pushes him forward and is never tired of him — and who could break in? Examine call and answer, and use the chink to see the chink; motion and change being clear, the awe can be divided. Knowing well the pattern of call and answer, one must know a chink where there is one; knowing it, one can see it; and seeing it, one can leave no chink oneself. So one is clear about motion and change, and the awe can be divided.

If you would move and change, you must first nourish the will and lay the intent low, and so watch for the chink. Able to nourish the will and lay the intent low, and to watch and know the chink, the art of moving and changing can be accomplished. Knowing what in you is firm and solid is nourishing yourself; yielding your own place is nourishing others. So with the spirit present and the weapons gone, one may then make the shape of the force. To know for oneself that will and intent are firm and solid is what one may nourish oneself by; to be able to practise ritual yielding upon oneself is what one may nourish others by. So the spirit is present within and the weapons gone without, and one may then make the shape of the force.

Scattering the Force, on the model of the Bird of Prey散勢法鷙鳥

Scattering the force, on the model of the bird of prey. The force scattered and then things submit, as the bird strikes and takes its quarry; hence "scattering the force, on the model of the bird of prey." Scattering the force is the spirit's errand. Force is loosed by the spirit, hence "force is the spirit's errand." In using it one must move along a chink. Without a chink the force does not run, so in using it one must move along a chink. When awe and gravity are full within and one pushes through the chink and makes it run, the force scatters. Awe and force being full within, and being loosed moreover through a chink, the force spreads and scatters of itself.

Now in scattering the force the heart is empty and the will brims over. The heart empty, there is nothing it does not hold; the will brimming, there is no affair it does not decide. So one can scatter one's force. Where the intent is spent and the awe lost and essence and spirit are not concentrated, the words are off the point and shift about. Will and intent spent and thin and the force lost, essence and spirit blunted and unconcentrated — then the words are loose and off the point and full of shifts. So observe his will and intent, and make of them measure and number; and then estimate and persuade and plan the affair, exhausting the round and the square and making even the short and the long. Knowing whether his will and intent are rising or falling, one makes measure and number for them. Measure and number once set, one estimates and persuades again. In planning the affair one must exhaust the pattern of round and square and even out the uses of short and long. Where there is no chink, do not scatter the force: ○ Collation: the Daoist Canon copy has 散 before 者. Turning the round is the endless plan. He who is endless must have the heart of a sage, to trace the unfathomable wit to its source and get through to the heart's art. Heaven and earth are without limit and human affairs without end. Each brings its kind to completion; see the design, and one must know where fortune and misfortune, success and failure, will end. Reducing and Seeing, on the model of the Sacred Milfoil損兊法靈蓍

Reducing and seeing, on the model of the sacred milfoil. The Laozi says: "Block up the dui." Heshang Gong says: "Dui is the eyes." The Zhuangzi says: "The heart has eyes." So dui here means examining the pattern with the heart's eye; and sun, "reducing," means cutting down other deliberation and examining with the heart alone. Dui can know gain and loss, and the milfoil can know weal and woe; hence "reducing and seeing, on the model of the sacred milfoil." Reducing and seeing is the deciding of the incipient and the perilous. The omen of the incipient and perilous, the minuteness of a pattern just moving, nothing but the heart's eye can examine and see; hence "reducing and seeing is the deciding of the incipient and the perilous." Affairs have their "it so happened"; things have their success and failure. The motion of the incipient and perilous must not go unexamined. "It so happened" means what comes about at one time or another. The success and failure of things comes about at one time or another; the motion of the incipient and perilous goes from the minute to the manifest. Unless one's feeling and recognition are far and deep, and one knows the incipient and deeply surveys, one cannot know what has not yet signalled itself nor examine what has not yet taken form; and then wind and wave rise in secret and the perilous spring is loosed unseen, and afterwards the capacity of rivers and seas is silted into a dry trickle and the heaping of one basket at a time piles into a mountain. Not having designed for it at the start, what use is regret? Hence "must not go unexamined."

So the sage waits, doing nothing, upon those who have virtue; he speaks, examines their wording, and matches it to their deeds. The sage is diligent in seeking worthy men and careful in employing them; so he sits with folded hands doing nothing, and waits upon the man of virtue. When such a man comes, he must lay out his case in words — hence "he speaks, examines their wording"; and he is further tested by results — hence "matches it to their deeds." Dui, seeing, is knowing it; sun, reducing, is doing it. Using the heart's eye, one can know it; cutting down other deliberation, one can do it. Where in reducing and speaking there is that in a thing which cannot be, the sage does not make words for it. Where in the persuasion of reducing, and in the thing persuaded of, the pattern makes it impossible, the sage does not produce words to argue it.

So the wise man does not let his own speech cost him the speech of others; and so his wording is not encumbered and his heart not empty, his will not disordered and his intent not crooked. The wise man hears the pleadings of the crowd and gathers the words of the grass-cutter and woodsman; though his discrimination compass all things he does not speak of himself, and so does not throw away others' words because he is able to speak. Using the words of the many, his wording is apt and unencumbered; giving the charge back to the hearts of the many, his heart is sincere and unfeigned. His heart sincere and his words apt, how should will and intent be disordered or crooked? ○ Collation: for 訟 the Daoist Canon copy has 頌; the two graphs were interchangeable in old writing. Lose the Way of what is so of itself and the affair is wrecked and the work falls short; so one must take that Way as the substance of one's designing.

What is round does not run and what is square does not stop: this is called the great work. Adding or reducing, one makes words for it. The marvel of design is to be able to turn calamity into blessing and make success out of defeat, to thwart the other and bring oneself through. Holding the Pivot持樞

The pivot sits at the centre and turns what is outside, stands near and governs what is far; it is master of turning and moving. So the north star of heaven is called the pivot of heaven, and the turning-post of a door is called the door's pivot. To hold the pivot, then, is to grasp the handle of movement and turning and by it govern things.

Holding the pivot: that spring gives birth and summer grows, autumn gathers in and winter stores away, is the rightness of heaven, It says that spring, summer, autumn and winter, the running of the four seasons, is unmade and so of itself; and being unmade and so of itself is what makes it right. and it may not be crossed and gone against. He who goes against it, though he succeed, must come to ruin. What the pattern must have, what things are of themselves — be still and go with it, and the four seasons run their course and the ten thousand things are born. But cross their season and go against their weather, and what is already accomplished comes to ruin — how much more what is not yet accomplished! Yuanliang says: "Of the breathing kinds, go with them and they are pleased, cross them and they are angry; and how much more heaven, that is honoured above all things — and shall one go against it?"

So the ruler of men too has his pivot of heaven: birth and nurture, completion and storing; The ruler of men models himself on heaven in his motion; hence "has too his pivot of heaven." And birth and nurture, completion and storing are the running of heaven's Way; the rightness of human affairs is no different. and this too may not be crossed and gone against. He who goes against it, though he flourish, must decay. This is the great mainstay of heaven's Way and of the ruler of men. To cross heaven's running and go against the rightness of men is what is called turning things upside down; so what should going against it be but decay? This art of holding the pivot is vexingly brief and cramped and does not open out the pattern to the end — perhaps the slips of the chapter were broken and lost, and the copy could not be made whole.

The Middle Canon中經

It means running out from the centre to order what is outside — issuing from the root of the heart and so stitching up the seams of things. Hence "the Middle Canon."

The Middle Canon means raising up the destitute and hastening to those in straits, and spending this upon men who can speak and men of ample virtue. Those held fast by circumstance and caught in extremity do not forget a kindness. Zhen is "to raise up"; qu is "to go toward." When men are in extremity and haste, one should raise them up and go to their aid; and in the spending of it, it must be upon men of speech and men of ample virtue. Save those held fast and caught, and the destitute cherish the virtue of it and never to the end forget the kindness. Those who can speak are of the company of the good and spread benefits wide. Chou is "kind" or "company." Men of speech untangle disputes and save men from hardship, and so do not fall out of the company of good men, and can carry kindness widely about. Those who dispense virtue go by the Way. The man who dispenses virtue moves along the pattern in what he does, and does not fall away from the Way. And he who saves those held fast nourishes small men and makes them serviceable. Where small men are held fast and one can save and nourish them, small men become serviceable.

A gentleman may meet an age out of joint: it may be his lot to be saved from the ditch by some chance; or to be cut down for being able to speak; or to see virtue broken and force made king; or to be held down and made a criminal; or to grieve and keep his own goodness; or to be ruined again and again and stand up for himself. "The ditch" is when there is war and calamity in the age and men are rolled into the ditches of the road; a gentleman may by some chance escape that. "Cut down for being able to speak": the way of small men grows long and the slanderers have no limit, so men of speech are often destroyed. "Virtue broken and force made king" is to wreck the civil virtue and honour arms and battle. "Held down and made a criminal" is a man who has done nothing and is bound in fetters all the same. "Grieve and keep his own goodness": when the world is a waste with no thread of order left, the worthy man holds to the good Way unto death and does not let his true heart alter — what is called "only when the year turns cold do we know that pine and cypress are the last to wither," and "though the wind and rain make it dark as night, the cock does not leave off crowing." "Ruined again and again and stands up for himself": heaven has not yet repented, and danger and ruin follow one another, but the gentleman in straits must come through and can stand up for himself in the end — as Guan Zhong did. ○ Collation: for 悔過 the Daoist Canon copy has 悔禍.

So in the Way what counts is to govern others and not to be governed by others. ○ Collation: the Qian copy has a graph missing after 於人. To govern others is to hold the handle; to be governed by others is to lose one's fate. What counts is to have the art and govern others, not to be without art and be governed by them.

And who can be master of the four notes but gong? Gong is earth. Earth reigns over the four seasons, and the other four are born by it; so it is master of the four notes. So where the notes do not harmonise there is grief; and when the voice is scattered, wounded, ugly or harmful, the words are sure to grate on the ear. Compiled by Qin Enfu 秦恩復 (Qing)

Passages quoted as Guiguzi in other books but absent from the text as we have it; the surviving scraps of an old preface; and the earliest accounts of the man himself.

Lost passages

Garden of Persuasions, "On Good Persuasion," quotes the Guiguzi: "To take a man who is not good and straighten him is hard. When a persuasion does not go through and the words are not followed, it is because the argument was not clear. Clear, and still it does not go through — then it was not held to firmly. Held to firmly, and still it does not go through — then it has not hit on what the man's own heart holds good. Make the argument clear, hold to it firmly, and hit besides on what the man holds good, and the words are divine and precious, white and distinct, and can enter into a man's heart. That such a persuasion should fail to go through has never been heard of in this world."

Shiji, "The Grand Historian's Own Preface": "So it is said: 'The sage does not decay; he keeps himself by the shifts of the time. Emptiness is the constant of the Way; going by things is the mainstay of the ruler.'" The Suoyin commentary says: "This comes from the Guiguzi; Qian quoted it to complete his chapter, hence 'so it is said.'" The Life of Sima Qian in the Han shu has 巧, "cunning," for 朽, "decay"; Yan Shigu says: "He has no cunning and contriving heart, but only goes with the time."

Shiji, Suoyin on the hereditary house of Tian, quotes the Guiguzi: "Tian Chengzi killed the ruler of Qi, and for twelve generations his line held Qi." Note: the wording is the same as in the "Prying the Chest" chapter of the Zhuangzi.

Taiping Yulan, section on the Way of governing, quotes the Guiguzi: "In serving a sage ruler there is listening and following, and no remonstrance and contention; in serving a middling ruler there is remonstrance and contention, and no flattery and fawning; in serving a violent ruler there is patching and trimming, and no straightening and thwarting." And again: "Let the ruler get a name and the whole body of ministers will lean on it."

Yilin quotes the Guiguzi: "When others move I am still; when others speak I listen. To hold fast or to let go — it rests with me, and I ask myself. Know your nature and your burdens are few; know your fate and you do not grieve. Grief and burden gone, the heart is level; the heart level, humaneness and right stand out." And again: "To nourish the people with virtue is like plants getting their season; to transform men with humaneness is like heaven giving birth to plants and soaking them with rain." The seven items above are lost passages of the Guiguzi.

From an old preface

The Wenxuan commentary quotes the preface to the Guiguzi: "In the Zhou age there was a bold gentleman who lived hidden in Ghost Valley and styled himself Guiguzi, meaning that he had put himself far away. But the name Ghost Valley is a general style for recluses."

Taiping Yulan, section on rites, quotes the Guiguzi: "In Zhou there was a bold gentleman who lived in Ghost Valley and was styled the Divine Master of Ghost Valley. Su Qin and Zhang Yi went to see him. The master said: 'I shall set out the utmost Way before you two; do you fast and purify yourselves and choose a day to learn.' Afterwards Qin and Yi fasted and purified themselves and went." This item is probably from the preface to the Guiguzi.

Chao Gongwu, Record of Readings, Yin Zhizhang's preface: "Yi and Qin came again to see him, and the master then straightened his mat and sat, composed his face and spoke, and told the two of them the Way of keeping the person whole."

Shiji, Suoyin on the life of Su Qin: "Yue Yi, annotating the book of Guiguzi, says: 'Su Qin wished to make his Way seem divine and secret, and so borrowed the name of Ghost Valley.'" The four items above belong to the preface to the Guiguzi.

Three Colophons鬼谷子跋

Colophon by Zhou Guangye周廣業跋

Master Bao Lüyin acquired a manuscript copy of the annotated Guiguzi and asked me to set it right. The commentary is very lucid, plain and apt, and plainly beyond the reach of men of the Five Dynasties or the Song. Yet the head of the scroll is inscribed "annotated by Master Zhenbai of the Eastern Jin, Tao Hongjing of Danyang" — and that is wrong. Tao belonged to the Liang, and was granted the posthumous name Zhenbai at the beginning of the Datong era; the error "Eastern Jin" needs no long argument.

Consider: the Guiguzi is catalogued from the Sui bibliography on, with commentaries by Huangfu Mi and Yue Yi in three scrolls each; the Old and New Tang bibliographies drop Huangfu and add Yin Zhizhang's commentary in three scrolls, and say nothing of Tao. Tao's commentary first appears in Chao Gongwu's Record of Readings, and Song Lian's Disputation of the Masters follows him. It has the same number of scrolls as Yue's and Yin's and lacks the two chapters Turning the Pellet and Prying the Chest; this copy's chapters and scrolls answer to that exactly, and it must be the one Mr Song saw.

The book is unlike the old copies. It puts Opening and Closing, Turning Back and Responding, Fastening Within and Striking the Fissure in the upper scroll; Flying and Clamping, Crossing and Joining, Estimating and Probing, Weighing and Designing, Deciding Affairs and Words as Tallies, together with the lost chapters, in the middle; and the Seven Arts of the Yin Tally of the Root Canon with Holding the Pivot and the Middle Canon in the lower — no different from recent printings. Of all the passages lost from the text but preserved in the Shiji, the Yilin, the Taiping Yulan and the rest, this copy has none. And the chapter titles once given as Turning and Returning, Striking the Fissure, Flying and Gripping, Blackening and Muffling, Crossing and Joining, Estimating Feeling, Probing Intent, Measuring the Weight and Design and Deliberation are not so here either.

As for the chapters Filling the Spirit, Nourishing the Will and the rest, they are precisely what Liu Zongyuan mocked when he said "at last the Seven Arts came out too, strange and false and past all checking." Could such things really have existed in the Liang? And even if they did, the Recluse held his will high at Huayang — what use had he for devious and tricky talk? Neither the History of the Liang nor the stele inscription for the Prince of Shaoling says a word about his annotating the Guiguzi; so how can the attribution be anything but false?

Wang Yinglin's Record of Studies records Yin Zhizhang's preface to the Guiguzi, which says: "Su Qin and Zhang Yi served him and received the thirteen chapters of the art of opening and closing, and received further the three chapters Turning the Pellet and Prying the Chest." Chao merely says that the preface calls this the book given to Qin and Yi. Full or brief, the two prove that both are Yin's preface. The preface being Yin's, why should the commentary not be Yin's too?

Look at the commentary and it constantly avoids Tang taboo-names: writing 人 for 民, 代 for 世, 理 for 治, 縲絏 for 縲紲, and the like; and the brushwork is moreover exactly like that of the Commentary on the Guanzi. That it is Yin's commentary is beyond doubt. Yin lived in the reigns of Zhongzong and Ruizong and died in the sixth year of Kaiyuan, and so no longer avoided the graphs of "Longji." His note on the lost chapters says: "Some have taken Zhuang Zhou's 'Prying the Chest' to fill out the sequence; as it is not the intent of this book, nothing is taken from it." His note on Holding the Pivot says: "Vexingly brief and cramped — perhaps the slips of the chapter were broken and lost, and the copy could not be made whole." After the sinking of the Dizhu barge the five sections were mutilated and the old Wende-hall copy could not be seen again, and so the commentators counted it a grief.

Had the commentary really been Tao's, then Liu Xie, his contemporary, in the Wenxin diaolong, says plainly: "Turning the Pellet gives its skilful phrasing free rein; Flying and Gripping keeps its fine art in reserve." Could a man who had seen the original text have said out of hand that Turning the Pellet was already lost? Yu Zhongrong too was a man of the Liang, and of the masters he excerpted there survive in the Yilin the two items "when others move I am still" and "to nourish the people with virtue," which plainly rest on a complete book. Why then should this copy alone lament the text as broken and lost? Here too is clear proof that it is Yin's and not Tao's.

How "Yin" was corrupted into "Tao" I cannot explain. Guessing at it: Yin's commentary is in the Old History, and is said to have circulated widely in its day; yet the New Bibliography notes of itself that "Yin Zhizhang is not entered in the catalogue," which suggests that at the beginning of the Song the copy carried no attribution at all. And in the notes to Holding the Pivot the name "Yuanliang says" occurs once. Yuanliang was the style of Tao Yuanming of the Jin; someone may have mistaken Tao Yuanming for Tao Tongming and so set up an owner's name out of nothing, which readers failed to check, and the loan became permanent. Or else some flatterer of the Way — having already falsely made Guiguzi out to be Wang Xu and forced on him the name Master Yuanwei — may have taken Zhenbai's fondness for the immortals' arts as a pretext and fathered the commentary on him. One cannot know.

But the commentary is now rarely transmitted and is much to be treasured, and it would seem right to re-title it "annotated by Yin Zhizhang, Erudite of the Imperial Academy under the Tang," and print it together with Zhao Rui's Changduan jing; the profit to men's wits would be no small thing.

The seventh day of the intercalary fifth month, xinchou of Qianlong (1781). Written by Zhou Guangye of Haining.

Colophon by Lu Wenchao盧文弨跋

The Guiguzi is a small man's book. Its arts of opening and closing, of hook and clamp, can be worked only on a benighted ruler. The intent is to sound out which way his mind leans, and then cunningly to shift one's own account of things to suit it and so tie him up, until he takes one for a favourite and cleaves fast and cannot be parted from one. In all ages the crooked and wicked who make their master out a fine fellow have done exactly this. Do they need to be taught it before they know it, or to study it before they can do it? That their arts agree one by one with this book does not mean they have all seen the book. Lai Hu said it: "Opening and closing, flying and clamping, are in truth the ordinary manners of our own day; men who never read the Guiguzi answer to it like two halves of a tally." That is true.

And when one looks at how it is applied to ordinary men, it too must be to the benighted, the crooked, the mediocre and the timid. The book says: "The man who keeps guard — whose eye does not look at what is wrong, whose ear does not listen to what is crooked, whose words are always of the Odes and Documents and whose conduct is not perverse or licentious, who makes the Way his form and virtue his countenance, whose look is grave and whose colour is warm — he cannot be got at by figuring his look. With such a man, hide your purpose, stop the chink and go away." On the showing of that passage, the art runs out when it meets an upright man. And the chapter Striking the Fissure says: "When the age offers nothing to strike at, he hides himself deep and waits his time." Is that not to say that when one meets an enlightened ruler and a well-governed age, the whole art one carries is of no use at all?

Someone asks: "If so, why is the book not destroyed?" I answer: every shape and condition of the crooked and wicked is fully displayed in it. He who is a ruler of men cannot but turn it over and keep it in mind, so that should he meet such a person he may see clean through his liver and lungs. The other means to try his art on me, and I am able to turn it back on him beforehand. That is helping the one above to know men — how could one destroy it?

What I much regret is that within his square inch, the lodging of the divine clarity, the man was wholly taken up with tricks and shifts and deceits, and in the end could not escape running dry. To what purpose all that trouble? And some of his arts are so shallow and so crude that they could not possibly move anyone. Take the passage on meeting a man given to wine and women: one is to make an art of music for him, on the supposition that it is enough to shift what he likes. But hearing correct music he will do nothing but fear he may fall asleep; so one would have to use loose and melting music to stir him at all — and loose, melting music is fit only to help his appetites along. Is the art not looser still?

This book was cut from the Daoist Canon copy by Qin Dunfu, Academician of Jiangdu, the son of a family long allied to mine, and I collated it once for him. This year, jiayin (1794), I saw for the first time Qian Zunwang's own manuscript copy, and so learned that the corruptions and omissions of the Canon text are past counting — in the chapter Fastening Within alone four hundred and twelve graphs of text and commentary have dropped out. I hastened to borrow it and repair the text by it. Ah! Had this copy not existed, should we not have gone on taking the Canon copy for a good one? The collation done, I have therefore written this at the end.

Colophon by Lu Wenchao of Dongli.

Colophon by Ruan Yuan阮元跋

Tao Hongjing's annotated Guiguzi is an old text of the Daoist Canon. Qin Dunfu, Compiler, of my own district, is widely read and fond of antiquity and exact in collation; he has therefore culled from many books, fixed the errors, and put it through the blocks. The outline of it is given in his own preface.

Reading the Guiguzi, I find much rhymed language in it. Moreover its chapter Striking the Fissure says: "A fissure is a crack," reading 巇 as hu — which agrees with the old phonetic glossing of graphs, and is not something a later hand could have faked. Among the chapter titles there is Flying and Clamping. Now in the Zhouli, office of spring, "Master of Concord," we read "a faint sound is an"; and the later Zheng reads this as the an of 飛鉆湼韽. 箝 and 鉆 are the same graph, and Jia Gongyan's sub-commentary cites the Guiguzi in evidence. As for the two chapters Estimating and Probing, they look to be modelled on the phrase in the life of Su Qin, "he drilled himself in estimating and probing." Yet the life of Yu Qing in the Shiji says that the Spring and Autumn of Mr Yu also had a chapter Estimating and Probing; so it was the common language of the roving persuaders.

I hold privately that any book catalogued in the Sui and Tang bibliographies and barely surviving now ought without exception to be exactly collated and handed on — and how much more this one, the sole surviving masters' book of the Vertical-and-Horizontal school, with a commentary by Tao long lost to the world besides. Those who gather up the old records will truly be glad to see it.

Winter, wushen of Qianlong (1788). Colophon by Ruan Yuan of Yizhen.

On the rendering
Chapter titles keep the Chinese alongside, because most of them are technical terms of the book rather than ordinary words — bai he 捭闔, fei qian 飛箝, di xi 抵巇. Recurring terms are rendered consistently: dao as "the Way," qi as "breath," shen as "spirit," shi as "force," wei as "awe," ji 幾 as "the incipient," quan as "weighing" or "the handle" as the sense requires.
