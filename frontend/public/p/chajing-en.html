Scroll One卷上

Three chapters on tea while it is still on the mountain and in the shed. Where the plant comes from, the fifteen implements for picking and pressing it, and how the cakes are made between the second month and the fourth. Read straight through, the three chapters follow one cake of tea from the branch to the sealed store. Note the order of Lu Yu’s argument: the ground first, the craftsman second. “Wild is best, garden-grown second” — he puts the grading in the soil, not in the workmanship. Workmanship can only fail to spoil what the ground gave.

I. The Origins一之源

Six paragraphs, one subject each: the tree, the written character, the names, the ground, the uses, and the harm. The last is the most characteristic. One paragraph praises tea; one paragraph warns against it, with ginseng for a comparison. A book written to promote tea ends its first chapter on the words “then all the harm tea can do has been told”.

Tea is a fine tree of the south. It grows to one foot, to two feet, even to some tens of feet. In the gorge country of the Ba mountains there are trees two men cannot reach around; they must be felled before the leaf can be gathered. The tree is like the gualu, the leaf like the gardenia, the flower like a white wild rose, the fruit like the fan palm, the stalk like clove, the root like walnut. 〔The gualu tree grows at Guangzhou. It resembles tea and is extremely bitter. The binglü is of the fan-palm kind; its seed is like tea. Walnut and tea both send their roots downward, splitting the gravel before the shoot will rise.〕

As to the written character, it may take the grass radical, or the wood radical, or both together. 〔With the grass radical it is written 茶, a form given in the Kaiyuan Dictionary of Characters and Sounds; with the wood radical it is written 𣗪, a form given in the Materia Medica; with grass and wood together it is written 荼, a form given in the Erya.〕

As to the names, the first is cha, the second jia, the third she, the fourth ming, the fifth chuan. 〔The Duke of Zhou says: “Jia is bitter tu.” Yang the Halberdier says: “The people of south-western Shu call tea she.” Guo of Hongnong says: “Picked early it is tu, picked late it is ming; some call it chuan.”〕

As to the ground: the best tea grows on weathered rock, the middling on gravelly soil, the poorest on yellow earth. Planting that is not firmed down, setting out that is not close, seldom comes to anything; the method is that of the melon, and there is picking in the third year. Wild is best, garden-grown second. On a sunlit slope under shading trees, purple shoots are better than green, spear-shaped better than bud-shaped, furled leaves better than open ones. Tea from a shaded hillside or valley is not worth picking: its nature is clogging, and it breeds an obstruction in the belly.

As a thing to use, tea is of the coldest nature, and as a drink it best suits a man of austere conduct and frugal virtue. For burning thirst, a clenched chest, a sore head, dry eyes, tired limbs, aching joints, four or five sips will hold their own against the finest cream and sweet dew itself.

Picked out of season, made without care, mixed with weeds, tea will make a man ill when he drinks it. The harm tea can do is like the harm ginseng can do. The best ginseng grows at Shangdang, the middling in Paekche and Silla, the poorest in Koguryŏ; what comes from Ze, Yi, You and Tan prefectures has no medicinal effect at all — and that is still ginseng. How much worse what is not. If a man takes jini root instead and his six ailments are not cured, then knowing what harm ginseng can do, he will know all the harm tea can do as well.

II. The Tools二之具

Fifteen implements for picking and pressing, in the order they are used: picking, steaming, pounding, moulding, drying, piercing, firing, counting, storing. These are not the vessels of Chapter IV — those are for boiling and drinking, and the Siku editors noted that Lu Yu deliberately keeps the two sets apart. Every item is given its material and its measurements: you could build them from this text.

The ying 〔pronounced ying〕, also called a basket, a cage, or a hamper, is woven of bamboo and holds five sheng, or one, two or three pecks. The tea-picker carries it on his back to gather tea. 〔Ying: the History of the Han gives its sound as ying, as in the saying “a basket full of gold is not worth one classic.” Yan Shigu’s note says: “The ying is a bamboo vessel; it holds four sheng.”〕

The stove: use one without a chimney. The cauldron: use one with a lipped mouth.

The steamer may be of wood or of earthenware. It should not be waisted, and the joint is sealed with clay. A bamboo tray inside holds the leaf, tied with a strip of split bamboo so that it can be lifted. When the steaming begins the leaf goes in on the tray; when it is done it comes out on the tray. When the cauldron boils dry, water is poured in through the steamer. 〔The steamer: no band, but sealed with clay.〕 A three-pronged branch of paper-mulberry is then used to spread out the steamed shoots and leaves, for fear the sap should run off.

The pestle and mortar, also called the pounder. The one in constant use is the best.

The mould, also called the form or the frame, is made of iron. It may be round, or square, or flower-shaped.

The bed, also called the stand or the anvil, is made of stone. Failing that, take a block of locust or mulberry wood and bury half of it in the ground, so that nothing can shift.

The cloth, also called the coat, is made of oiled silk, or of a worn-out raincoat or unlined gown. The cloth is laid on the bed and the mould set on the cloth, and so the cake is pressed. When the cake is formed, cloth and all are lifted away and another laid down.

The drying tray 〔pronounced pali〕, also called the little basket or the pangláng, is made on two small bamboos three feet long, with a body two feet five inches across and a handle five inches long. Split bamboo is woven into square eyes, like the sieve a gardener uses for earth, two feet wide, and on this the cakes are laid out.

The awl, also called the piercing knife. The handle is of hard wood, and it is used to pierce the cakes.

The cord, also called the whip, is made of bamboo. The cakes are strung on it so that they can be taken off one by one.

The drying pit is dug two feet deep, two feet five inches wide and ten feet long, with a low wall two feet high built over it and sealed with clay.

The skewer is of split bamboo, two feet five inches long; the cakes are threaded on it to be dried over the pit.

The rack, also called the stage, is built of wood over the drying pit, in two tiers a foot apart, for drying the tea. Half-dried tea goes up onto the lower tier; fully dried, it moves to the upper.

The string 〔pronounced chuàn〕 is made of split bamboo in Jiangdong and Huainan, and of twisted paper-mulberry bark in the gorge country of Ba. In Jiangdong one jin makes a great string, half a jin a middle string, four or five ounces a small string. In the gorges a hundred and twenty jin make a great string, eighty a middle string, fifty a small one. The character used to be written 釧, as in a bracelet, or 串, as in a skewer; it is not so now. Like the five characters grind, fan, pluck, bore and sew, it is written in the level tone and spoken in the departing tone, and as a name for this thing it is written 穿.

The store is framed in wood, latticed in bamboo and papered over. There is a partition inside, a lid above, a floor below and a door at the side with a single leaf. A vessel is set inside holding banked embers, so that a gentle warmth is kept up. In the plum rains of the south the fire is lit. 〔It is called the store because it stores and keeps the tea alive.〕

III. The Making三之造

The process, and how to judge the result. The seven operations take fourteen characters: picked, steamed, pounded, moulded, fired, pierced, sealed. But a whole paragraph goes to the eight appearances a cake of tea may have, and another to the three levels of connoisseurship — the man who can only praise ranks lowest, the man who can only fault ranks second, and the man who can do both ranks first. This is the book’s central passage on judgement.

Tea is picked in the second, third and fourth months.

The spear-shaped shoots grow on weathered rock and rich soil, four or five inches long, like bracken just uncoiling, and are picked while the dew is on them. The bud-shaped grow out of the thickets, in sprays of three, four or five; pick the tallest sprig from the middle. On a day of rain there is no picking; on a clear day with cloud there is no picking. In clear weather it is picked, steamed, pounded, moulded, fired, pierced and sealed, and the tea is dry.

Tea has ten thousand appearances. To speak roughly: some is puckered like a Hu tribesman’s boot 〔the crease a scribe’s awl makes〕; some pleated like the dewlap of a humped ox; some coiled like cloud coming off a mountain; some rippled like water under a light wind. Some is as though a potter had settled fine clay in water 〔that is, levigated clay〕; some as though new-turned ground had been crossed by a sudden flood. All these are the rich and excellent teas. Some is like bamboo sheath, the stems hard and solid, stubborn to steam and to pound, so that the cake comes out coarse as a sieve 〔the characters are written with 离 above and 師 below〕. Some is like a frosted lotus, stalk and leaf withered and changed out of shape, so that the cake looks shrunken and spent. These are the lean and old teas.

From the picking to the sealing there are seven operations; from the boot to the frosted lotus there are eight grades. To call a tea fine because it is glossy, black, flat and even is the lowest kind of judgement; to call it fine because it is wrinkled, yellow and pitted is the second kind. To be able to say both what is fine in it and what is not — that is the first kind. Why? Because tea whose sap has come out is glossy, and tea that holds its sap is wrinkled; tea made overnight is black, tea made in a day is yellow; tea pressed after steaming is flat and even, tea left to itself is pitted. In this tea is no different from any other leaf. Whether a tea is good or bad is a thing handed down by word of mouth.

Scroll Two卷中

One chapter, and the weightiest in the book: twenty-four vessels for making tea, from the brazier that holds the fire to the great basket that carries the whole set away. Each is given its material, its measurements and its reason. This is the earliest complete inventory of a set of implements in Chinese; every object on a later tea table traces back into this list. The order matters — it is not by size but by action: light the fire, break the charcoal, set the cauldron, toast the cake, grind it, measure the water, strain it, ladle it, salt it, pour out, wash up, lay out, pack up.

IV. The Vessels四之器

Twenty-four vessels. Lu Yu sets each name on a line of its own with the description under it — that is how the old editions print it, and this page keeps it by putting the name in bold at the head of the paragraph. The sub-entries (the ash tray under the brazier, the feather brush under the mill, the salt spoon under the salt jar, the paper wrappers under the basket) are also the original’s. Two entries show the man most clearly: the brazier, whose three feet carry twenty-one characters of the Book of Changes, and the bowl, where he argues Yue ware above Xing ware in three rounds. His reason is “green sets off the tea”: a vessel is judged in the end by what it does to the tea.

The brazier 〔with its ash tray〕 The brazier is cast in bronze or iron in the shape of an ancient tripod, three-tenths of an inch thick in the wall and nine-tenths broad at the rim, leaving six-tenths hollow, and the inside is plastered. It has three feet, and on them twenty-one characters in archaic script. One foot reads: “Kan above, Xun below, Li in the middle.” One foot reads: “Balance the five phases and be rid of the hundred ills.” One foot reads: “Cast in the year after the sage Tang destroyed the Hu.” Between the three feet three windows are cut, and one window in the floor, to draw the draught and let the ash fall. Above them are six more characters in archaic script: over one window 伊公, over the next 羹陸, over the next 氏茶 — that is, “Lord Yi’s broth, Mr Lu’s tea.” Inside stands a clay shelf, divided into three compartments. In one is a pheasant, a creature of fire, marked with the trigram Li; in one a tiger, a creature of wind, marked with Xun; in one a fish, a creature of water, marked with Kan. Xun rules the wind, Li the fire, Kan the water; wind raises fire and fire boils water, and so all three trigrams are present. It is ornamented with linked petals, hanging vines, running water and squared patterns. The brazier may be forged of iron or worked in clay. Its ash tray has three feet and stands on an iron dish.

The charcoal basket is woven of bamboo, a foot two inches high and seven inches across. It may also be of rattan: make a wooden last in the shape of the basket and weave over it with hexagonal eyes, and finish the base and the lid as one finishes the mouth of a bamboo box.

The charcoal mallet is made of iron, hexagonal, a foot long, pointed at the head and thick in the middle. A small ring is fastened at the thin end by way of ornament, like the truncheon the soldiers of Hexi and Longyou carry. It may be made in the form of a hammer or of an axe, whichever comes to hand.

The tongs are the ordinary fire-chopsticks, round and straight, a foot three inches long, cut off flat at the top, with no onion-bulb head and no linking chain. They are made of iron or of refined bronze.

The cauldron 〔pronounced fu; also written 釜 or 鬴〕 The cauldron is cast in pig iron — what the founders of our day call quick iron, made by melting down worn-out ploughshares and recasting them. The inner mould is of clay and the outer of sand. Clay makes the inside smooth, so that it washes easily; sand makes the outside rough, so that it takes the flame. The ears are made square, for uprightness. The rim is made wide, for reach. The belly is made deep, for holding the centre. A deep belly means the boil is in the middle; a boil in the middle means the tea powder rises easily; and when the powder rises easily the flavour is full. At Hongzhou they make them of porcelain and at Laizhou of stone. Porcelain and stone are both elegant, but they are not strong and will not last. Made of silver it is perfectly clean, but it verges on extravagance. Elegant it may be, and clean it may be; but for daily use one comes back to silver in the end.

The stand is crossed in the shape of the character for ten, hollowed out in the middle, and holds the cauldron up.

The tongs for toasting are made of small green bamboo, a foot two inches long, with an inch of it left jointed and the rest split, and with them the cake is toasted. The sap of young bamboo comes out over the fire, and its clean fragrance is added to the taste of the tea — though away from wooded valleys there is no getting it. Fine iron or refined bronze may be used instead, for their durability.

The paper bag is of thick white Shan vine paper sewn double. In it the freshly toasted tea is kept, so that the fragrance cannot escape.

The mill 〔and the brush〕 The mill is made of orange wood, or failing that of pear, mulberry, paulownia or cudrania. It is round within and square without: round within so that the wheel may run, square without so that it cannot topple. The trough holds the wheel with no wood to spare. The wheel is shaped like a cartwheel, with an axle but no spokes. The mill is nine inches long and one inch seven tenths wide; the wheel is three inches eight tenths across, one inch thick at the centre and half an inch at the rim, and the axle is square in the middle and round at the grip. The brush for sweeping up the powder is made of a bird’s feather.

The sieve and box The sifted powder is kept in the lidded box with the measure laid inside it. The sieve is made by splitting a large bamboo and bending it round, covered with fine silk gauze. The box is made from a section of bamboo, or of bent and lacquered cedar, three inches high, with a lid of one inch and a base of two, and a mouth four inches across.

The measure is made of a sea shell, an oyster or a clam, or of bronze, iron or bamboo in the form of a spoon or a strip. To measure is to weigh, to set a standard, to keep a proportion. The rule is one square-inch spoonful of powder to one sheng of water boiled. Those who like it weak take less, those who like it strong take more — and that is why it is called the measure.

The water tub is jointed from chou wood, locust, catalpa or zelkova, with every seam lacquered inside and out, and holds one peck.

The filter bag in ordinary use has a frame cast of raw copper, since it is always wet and raw copper grows no scum and gives no rank or harsh taste: refined copper grows scum and iron tastes rank. Those who live retired in hills and valleys sometimes use bamboo or wood, but neither will last a long journey, and so raw copper is used. The bag itself is woven of green bamboo rolled up, sewn with blue-green silk and studded with kingfisher-feather ornaments. It is carried in a bag of green oiled cloth. It is five inches across and the handle is an inch and a half.

The ladle is also called the xi dipper. It is made by splitting a gourd, or carved from wood. Du Yu of the Jin, a secretary of the palace, says in his Rhapsody on Tea: “Ladle it out with a gourd.” The gourd is the ladle. Its mouth is wide, its neck thin, its handle short. In the Yongjia period Yu Hong of Yuyao went up Waterfall Mountain to pick tea and met a Daoist who said: “I am Master Danqiu. I ask that some day you spare me what is left in the bowl and the dipper.” The xi is a wooden dipper. Nowadays they are commonly made of pear wood.

The bamboo stirrer is made of peach, willow or fan-palm wood, or of the heartwood of persimmon. It is a foot long and bound with silver at both ends.

The salt jar 〔and its spoon〕 The salt jar is made of porcelain, four inches across, shaped like a box, or sometimes like a bottle or a jar, and holds the salt flower. The spoon is of bamboo, four inches one tenth long and nine tenths wide. The spoon is a flat strip.

The hot-water bowl holds boiled water; it is of porcelain or earthenware and takes two sheng.

The bowl Yue ware is first; Ding, Wu and Yue prefectures next; Shou and Hong next again. Some put Xing ware above Yue, which is not so at all. If Xing ware is like silver, Yue ware is like jade: Xing is not the equal of Yue, first. If Xing ware is like snow, Yue ware is like ice: Xing is not the equal of Yue, second. Xing ware is white and makes the tea look red; Yue ware is green and makes the tea look green: Xing is not the equal of Yue, third. Du Yu of the Jin, in his Rhapsody on Tea, says: “Choose the vessel, pick the ware, out of eastern Ou.” Ou is Yue. The Yue bowl is best: the lip does not curl out, the foot curls and is shallow, and it holds half a sheng or less. Yue and Yue-prefecture wares are green, and green sets off the tea, giving it a white-and-red colour. Xing ware is white and the tea looks red; Shou ware is yellow and the tea looks purple; Hong ware is brown and the tea looks black. None of them suits tea.

The bowl basket 〔and its paper wrappers〕 The basket is plaited of coiled white rush and holds ten bowls; a charcoal basket will do instead. The paper wrappers are of Shan paper sewn double and cut square, and there are ten of them too.

The brush is made by combing out fan-palm bark, clamping it between pieces of dogwood and binding it, or by cutting a length of bamboo and sheathing it, so that it looks like a great writing brush.

The slop tub holds the water left from rinsing. It is jointed of catalpa wood, made like the water tub, and holds eight sheng.

The dregs tub gathers the dregs. It is made like the slop tub and holds five sheng.

The cloths are of coarse silk, two feet long. Make two of them and use them by turns to keep the vessels clean.

The stand is made as a bench or as a rack, of wood alone or bamboo alone, or of wood and bamboo painted yellow and black and lacquered so that it will fold. It is three feet long, two feet wide and six inches high. It is called the display stand because it gathers all the vessels in and sets them all out.

The great basket takes its name from holding every vessel there is. The inner layer is woven of bamboo strips in triangular square eyes; over it broad strips are laid double for the warp and narrow single strips bind them, pressing across the doubled warp in turn to make square eyes, so that the whole is open and fine. It is a foot five inches high; the base is a foot wide and two inches deep; it is two feet four inches long and two feet wide.

Scroll Three卷下

Six chapters. Boiling and drinking; then the whole written record of tea before the Tang, gathered into one chapter; then the tea districts of the empire; then what may be left out in the open air; and last, a single paragraph telling the reader to copy the other nine chapters onto silk and hang them up. Chapter VII takes more than half the scroll. Lu Yu is not arguing there; he is laying out the evidence first — forty-seven passages from medical books, dictionaries, local gazetteers, poems, family histories, ghost stories and an imperial will, running from the Divine Farmer to his own dynasty. It is the earliest documentary history of tea in existence.

V. The Boiling五之煮

The whole operation: toasting, grinding, choosing fuel, choosing water, the three boils, pouring out, and the taste. The three boils are this chapter’s law — fish eyes for the first, a string of pearls at the rim for the second, rolling waves for the third; past that the water is “old” and is not to be drunk. Salt goes in at the first boil, a ladleful is drawn off at the second and the powder cast in, and at the third that ladleful goes back to quiet the water and raise the froth. The closing passage on the froth runs through six images — jujube flower, duckweed, cloud, green coin, chrysanthemum petals, drifted snow.

In toasting the cake, take care not to do it where the wind blows the embers about: the flame darts like an awl and the heat is uneven. Hold the cake close to the fire and keep turning it. When blisters come up on it 〔pao, pronounced pao〕 like the back of a toad, draw it five inches off. When it has curled and opened out again, go back to the beginning and toast it once more. If the cake was dried by fire, stop when the steam comes through; if it was dried in the sun, stop when it turns supple.

At the outset, with the tenderest tea, it is pounded hot as soon as it comes off the steam; the leaf breaks up but the spear-shaped shoot holds. Set a strong man to it with a pestle of a thousand catties and he will not break it: it is like rolling a bead on lacquer — the strongest hand cannot hold it still. When it is finally done it seems to have neither pith nor bone. Toasted, its joints go soft as an infant’s arm. Then, while it is still hot, it goes into the paper bag, so that the finest of its fragrance cannot get away, and when it is cold it is ground. 〔Of ground tea, the best has grains like fine rice; the worst has grains like water-chestnuts.〕

For the fire, charcoal is best and hard wood next. 〔That is, mulberry, locust, paulownia or oak.〕 Charcoal that has been used for roasting and has taken up the smell of fat, and resinous woods, and broken-up old furniture, are not to be used. 〔Resinous woods are cypress, cassia and juniper; broken-up furniture means discarded utensils.〕 The men of old spoke of the taste of worn-out firewood, and they were right.

For water, mountain water is best, river water next, well water last. 〔The Rhapsody on Tea says: “For water, the streams of Min — draw from that clear flow.”〕 Of mountain water, take the milky springs and the slow flow from rock pools. Do not drink the water that leaps and races: drunk long it gives a swelling in the neck. Where water has branched off into the valleys and stands without draining away, from the height of summer until the frosts a hidden dragon may have left his poison in it; the drinker should cut a channel to let the bad water run off, and dip only when the new spring comes trickling through. River water should be taken far from where men live, and well water from a well much used.

When the bubbles are like fish eyes and there is the least sound, that is the first boil. When the rim bubbles like a welling spring strung with pearls, that is the second boil. When the waves roll and beat, that is the third boil. Beyond that the water is old and not to be drunk. At the first boil, salt is added to the measure of the water, and the mouthful tasted is thrown away 〔chuo means to taste〕 — or would one be leaving it flat and then chasing after the single taste of salt? 〔The two characters mean tasteless.〕 At the second boil, draw off a ladle of water, and with the bamboo stirrer make a ring in the heart of the liquid and cast the measured powder into the middle of it. In a little while the water surges up and spatters; then pour back the ladle drawn off to check it, and so raise the froth.

In pouring out, let the froth be shared evenly among the bowls. 〔The dictionaries and the Materia Medica say that bo is the froth of tea.〕 The froth is the flower of the liquor. The thin is called mo, the thick bo, the fine and light hua, the bloom. The bloom floats like jujube flowers on a round pool; like the first duckweed on a winding backwater; like the mackerel cloud of a clear bright day. The thin froth is like green coins of moss afloat at the water’s edge, or chrysanthemum petals fallen into the wine cups. The thick froth comes of boiling the dregs again: at the boil it piles flower on flower and froth on froth, white and heaped like drifted snow. The Rhapsody on Tea has it: “bright as banked snow, brilliant as the spring bloom” — and so it is.

At the first boil there is a film on the water like black mica, and it must be thrown off, or the taste will not be true. The first bowl is called juanyong, the lasting savour 〔the characters are pronounced jun and yong. What is most delicious is called juanyong: juan is savour, yong is lasting; a savour that lasts is juanyong. The History of the Han records that Kuai Tong wrote a work in twenty chapters called Juanyong.〕 It may be kept in the hot-water bowl, ready to raise the froth and to check the boil. The first bowl, then the second and third, come in that order. Beyond the fourth and fifth, unless a man is very thirsty, he should not drink. From one sheng of water, pour five bowls. 〔The bowls should be three at fewest and five at most. If there are as many as ten people, add a second brazier.〕 Drink them hot, one after another, for the heavy and turbid settle to the bottom and the fine and excellent float above. Let it go cold and the excellence goes off with the steam; and it is the same if you drink and drink and never finish.

Tea is frugal by nature and does not take to being spread thin: spread thin, its taste goes dull. Even with a bowl filled to the brim, half drunk the savour is already scant — how much more when there is more of it. Its colour is pale yellow. Its fragrance is shi 〔fragrance at its finest is called shi; the character is pronounced shi〕. When the taste is sweet it is jia; when it is not sweet but bitter it is chuan; when it is bitter in the mouth and sweet in the throat it is cha. 〔The Materia Medica says: what is bitter and not sweet is jia; what is sweet and not bitter is chuan.〕

VI. The Drinking六之飲

From why one drinks at all to what counts as drinking badly. It opens by setting drink among living things: whatever flies, runs or speaks lives by drinking. Then a pedigree for tea — from the Divine Farmer, through Yan Ying, Yang Xiong, Sima Xiangru, Wei Yao, Liu Kun, Zhang Zai, Lu Na, Xie An, Zuo Si — down to the present, when it is drunk in every house. Then the backbone of the chapter: the Nine Difficulties, each named in one word and answered with one example of getting it wrong.

What has wings flies; what has fur runs; what opens its mouth speaks. All three live between heaven and earth, and all three live by drinking and eating. Far-reaching indeed is the meaning of drink in its season! For thirst, one drinks a gruel; to dispel care and anger, one drinks wine; to sweep away torpor and sleep, one drinks tea.

Tea as a drink began with the Divine Farmer and was made known by the Duke of Zhou in Lu. In Qi there was Yan Ying; in the Han, Yang Xiong and Sima Xiangru; in Wu, Wei Yao; in the Jin, Liu Kun, Zhang Zai, my distant forebear Lu Na, Xie An and Zuo Si — all of them drank it. Flooding over the age and soaking into custom, it is at its height under the present dynasty: in the two capitals and between Jing and Yu it is drunk in every house in the street.

There is coarse tea, loose tea, powdered tea and cake tea; it is chopped, simmered, roasted or pounded, put into a jar or a crock and drowned in boiling water, and this is called steeped tea. Some add onion, ginger, jujube, orange peel, dogwood or mint, boil it through a hundred boils, ladle it up and down to make it smooth, or skim off the froth. This is the water they throw into the gutter — and yet the custom will not stop.

Alas! Heaven raises the ten thousand things and each has its own perfection; but the skill of men goes hunting only after what is easy and near at hand. A roof is what shelters us, and roofs are brought to perfection; clothes are what we wear, and clothes are brought to perfection; food and drink are what fill us, and both food and wine are brought to perfection. Tea has nine difficulties: first the making, second the judging, third the vessels, fourth the fire, fifth the water, sixth the toasting, seventh the grinding, eighth the boiling, ninth the drinking. Picked in cloud and dried at night is not making. Chewing for the taste and sniffing for the scent is not judging. A rank cauldron and a tainted bowl are not vessels. Greasy wood and kitchen charcoal are not fire. Racing torrents and standing floods are not water. Cooked outside and raw within is not toasting. Grinding it to a blue-green dust is not grinding. Working it clumsily and stirring it in haste is not boiling. Drinking in summer and giving it up in winter is not drinking.

Where the tea is rare and fresh and richly fragrant, there should be three bowls; where it is a grade below, five. If the company at table comes to five, pour three bowls; if to seven, pour five. With six or fewer, do not count the bowls at all; reckon simply that one person is short, and let the bowl of lasting savour make up for the one who lacks.

VIII. The Districts八之出

The tea country of the empire, graded. Eight circuits; in the first five every prefecture is ranked best, next, low or lowest, and the notes name the counties, mountains and monasteries and say which other district the tea resembles — a working comparison table, the most practical thing in the book. The last paragraph is honest: of twelve further prefectures Lu Yu says plainly that he does not know, that tea is occasionally got from them, and that it is said to be very good.

IX. The Abridgements九之略

When the rules may be relaxed. Picking and pressing in a mountain temple, boiling on a rock among the pines — the vessels that may be dispensed with are struck off one by one. Then the last sentence takes it all back: in a walled town, at a great man’s gate, let one of the twenty-four be missing and the tea is ruined. The two halves are meant to be read against each other: what may be left out on the mountain may not be left out in the city.

As to the implements for making: if it is the season of the cold-food fast in spring, and the tea is gathered by many hands at a mountain temple or a hill garden, and steamed and pounded there and dried again over the fire, then the awl, the cord, the drying pit, the skewer, the rack, the string and the store — seven things — may all be dispensed with.

As to the vessels for boiling: if there is a rock among the pines to sit on, the display stand may go. If dry firewood and a tripod are used, the brazier, the ash tray, the charcoal mallet, the tongs and the stand may go. If one is beside a spring or a stream, the water tub, the slop tub and the filter bag may go. If there are five people or fewer and the tea is already finely ground, the sieve and box may go. If one climbs by the vines and scales the cliff, or is hauled into a cave by a rope, and toasts and grinds the tea at the mouth of the hill, or keeps it wrapped in paper and boxed, then the mill and the brush may go. If the ladle, the bowls, the stirrer, the brush, the hot-water bowl and the salt jar will all go into one hamper, the great basket may go.

But within the walls of a city, at the gate of a prince or a duke, let one of the twenty-four vessels be missing and the tea is ruined.

X. The Scroll十之圖

The shortest chapter, one paragraph long. Copy the other nine chapters onto four or six widths of plain silk and hang them beside your seat, and the whole of the Classic of Tea stands before the eye. The tenth chapter is not a set of illustrations; it is an instruction to turn the book into a thing on the wall.

Take four widths of plain silk, or six, and write the foregoing out on them in sections, and set them at the corner of your seat; and then the origins of tea, its tools, its making, its vessels, its boiling, its drinking, its record, its districts and its abridgements stand before the eye and are kept there, and with that the Classic of Tea is complete from beginning to end.

Text and Collation

Base text. The punctuated Chajing in three scrolls and ten chapters on Chinese Wikisource, descending from the Bao Shigong family copy of Zhejiang that the Siku quanshu editors used; about 7,000 characters including Lu Yu’s own interlinear notes. The division into scrolls and chapters and Lu Yu’s notes belong to the book; the paragraph breaks, the bold names of the twenty-four vessels in Chapter IV, and the 〔 〕 brackets marking the notes are ours. In the old editions the notes are cut as double columns of small characters inside the running text; here they are set a size smaller and in another colour, with 〔 〕 for boundaries — the same treatment this shelf gives Zhao Rui’s notes in the Fanjing. Those notes are part of the text and are not governed by the annotation button on the Chinese pages; that button governs only the scroll, chapter and paragraph notes written for this edition. The book has no headings below the chapter, and this page does not invent any.

First collation witness: the Wenyuan Ge Siku quanshu. Two independent transcriptions were used — the WYG branch of Kanripo KR3i0019 and the Chinese Wikisource Chajing (Siku quanshu edition). Where two transcriptions of one edition disagree, that is an error of transcription and not a variant of the edition. Compared character by character over 7,116 characters, they disagree nowhere. Zero by itself does not prove independence — copying also gives zero — but the two mark different characters as illegible, so neither is a copy of the other. Collating the base text against the Siku text gives 139 variants at 197 character positions; a further 330 differences of graphic form, judged by the CJKVI variant tables, are not counted as variants.

Second witness, read by eye: a Ming printing of the Baichuan xuehai. The National Diet Library copy of the Chajing in three scrolls from the Baichuan xuehai, ninth collection (shelfmark 特1-2229; the scan is on Wikimedia Commons, PD-scan). The Baichuan xuehai was compiled by Zuo Gui in the Xianchun period of the Song, and the earliest surviving line of the Chajing runs through that collection; this copy is a Ming reprinting of it, and belongs to no Siku line. No transcription of it exists online, so the leaves were called up and read one by one, and every place where the base text and the Siku text disagree was looked at. Where this page says “the Baichuan text reads X”, that is our own reading of the scan, not a published transcription — please keep the distinction in mind. The cover image is the opening leaf of Scroll One of that very copy.

The Baichuan text carries its own corrections, and the base text has swallowed them. The Baichuan printing has small editorial notes marked with a circle inside the running text: under 「又以穀木枝三亞者製之」 in Chapter II it reads “〇亞 should be 椏, a forked branch”, and under 「兩都并荊俞間」 in Chapter VI, “〇俞 should be 渝, that is Bayu”. The base text simply prints 椏 and 渝 in the running text: the corrections have been quietly folded in and the trace is gone. This page follows the base text — the corrections are right — but records the fact here. A punctuated text that reads smoothly is not necessarily a smooth original.

Rule for emending. A reading is changed only when three conditions hold together: (1) the Baichuan text and the Siku text both differ from the base text; (2) the base text will not construe, or is plainly a slip for a graphically similar character; (3) there is outside evidence — the source quoted, or the book’s own testimony. Sixteen places meet the rule, each noted at its paragraph. Among them: in Chapter II 簞 and 薎 corrected to 箄 and 篾 (the next clause reads 「出乎箄」), three occurrences of 赤 to 尺, and a dropped 穿; in Chapter IV the sub-entry 拂末 restored under the mill and 具到者 corrected to 具列者; in Chapter V 茶罷熱搗 to 蒸罷熱搗 and 樌 to 檟; in Chapter VI 間於魯周公 to 聞; in Chapter VII 傳巽 to 傅巽 and 烏啄 to 烏喙 (aconite, one drug in a list of drugs); in Chapter VIII 昍仙茗 to 曰仙茗. Eight occurrences of 灸 for 炙, “to toast”, are corrected together rather than one by one; both old editions read 炙.

Nineteen characters missing from the base text, on Huzhou. In the Zhexi paragraph of Chapter VIII the base text runs straight from 「生長城縣顧渚山谷，與峽州、荊州、義陽郡同」 to 「生鳳亭山……」, dropping the clause “what grows at the two monasteries of Shanshang and Rushi and on Xuanjiao Ridge at Baimao Mountain is the same as Xiangzhou, Jingnan and Yiyang tea”. The Baichuan printing has it in full and the Siku text keeps its shape with some corruption. It has been restored, and with it the readings 「與峽州、光州同」 above and 「與壽州、常州同」 below, both on the authority of the two old editions. Huzhou is where Lu Yu wrote the book, and Guzhu Mountain became the first tribute-tea mountain of the Tang: the most consequential geographical passage in the book is the one the base text had lost most of.

“Cast in the year after the sage Tang destroyed the Hu” — the Siku editors replaced those words. The third of the three inscriptions on the brazier’s feet in Chapter IV reads, in the base text, 「聖唐滅胡明年鑄」, which dates the brazier to 764, the year after the An Lushan rebellion was put down. The Wenyuan Ge Siku text reads 「聖唐年號某年鑄」, “cast in such-and-such a year of the reign of the sage Tang” — and both transcriptions agree, so it is not a slip of transcription. We examined this leaf of the Baichuan printing: after 唐 there stands a single character, then 明年鑄, and the impression is worn enough that we will not say what character it is. The base text is therefore kept and all three readings recorded here. 胡 was a word to be avoided under the Qing, and the Siku editors altered such wordings often; we state the fact and leave the inference alone.

Two matters in which the Siku text stands alone. First, the chapter titles: the Siku text reads 一茶之源, 二茶之具, 四茶之噐 and so on throughout, where the base text and the Baichuan printing both read 一之源, 二之具, 四之器. The words 「一之源」 are perfectly clear on the cover image, and this page follows them. Second, the list of vessels: the Siku text prefixes Chapter IV with a table of the twenty-four vessels; the Baichuan printing has none, and neither has the base text, and this page does not supply one. A third small thing, also visible on the cover: the Baichuan printing names the author 「唐　竟陵陸羽」, “Lu Yu of Jingling, of the Tang”, where the Siku text has 「唐　陸羽　撰」.

The Siku text is a poor witness, and that should be said plainly. Most of the variants found in this round are simply its errors. In Chapter VII, Tao Hongjing’s 「輕身換骨」 becomes 換膏 and 「黃山君」 becomes 責山君; the Further Lives of Eminent Monks passage 「元嘉中過江，遇沈臺真，請真君武康小山寺」 becomes 「永嘉中……遇沈臺真臺在武康小山寺」; Fu Xian’s 「爲簾事打破其器具，後又賣餅於市」 becomes 「爲羣吏打破其器具，嗣又賣餅於市」; Liu Kun’s 「吾體中憒悶」 becomes 潰悶; 「臨蒸縣」 becomes 臨遂縣; and in the note on juanyong the name of Kuai Tong is left blank altogether. We therefore treat it as an independent witness and not as a good text to emend from: for each of the sixteen emendations the Baichuan printing had to agree.

Ten places where both old editions differ from the base text and we have not changed it, listed here so that readers may judge for themselves. Chapter I: 「中者生礫壤」, both old editions 櫟壤. Chapter III: 「故厥狀委悴然」, both 委萃. Chapter IV: 「銳上豐中」, both 銳一豐中; 「紐翠鈿以綴之」, both 細翠鈿; 「器擇陶簡」, both 陶揀; 「晉舍人杜育」, both 杜毓. Chapter V: 「菊英墮於鐏俎」, both 樽俎; 「或留熟盂以貯之」, neither has the character 盂. Chapter VII: 「八公山沙門曇濟」, both 譚濟. Chapter VIII: 「襄州生南漳縣」, both 南鄭; 「梁州生褒城」, both 襄城. In all ten the base text construes, and mostly agrees with the standard modern critical texts, so it stands.

One place where all three differ, and nothing was done. Chapter IX, 「乃蒸，乃舂，乃拍，乃復以火乾之」: the Baichuan printing has no 乃拍 (it reads 乃蒸乃舂乃復以火乾之) and the Siku text reads 乃煬 (乃蒸乃舂乃煬以火乾之). Three editions, three readings, no two agreeing, so the base text stands. The converse case is worth noting too: in Chapter VII the Spring and Autumn of Master Yan is quoted as 「炙三戈五卯」, where the Siku text reads 「三弋五卵」, agreeing with the received text of the Yanzi chunqiu — but the Baichuan printing still reads 戈 and 卯, so the two old editions do not agree and nothing was changed.

A misbound leaf in the NDL copy. This Baichuan xuehai printing carries at the front a 「茶經序」, in fact Pi Rixiu’s preface to his Miscellaneous Poems on Tea (“According to the Rites of Zhou, the office of Superintendent of Wine distinguishes four kinds of drink, the third being gruel… Ji Ci was the first to make a classic of it in three scrolls, dividing its origins, ordering its tools…”). In this copy the leaf is bound between the first and second leaves of Scroll One, so that Chapter I breaks off in the middle, the preface runs its course, and Chapter I resumes. The preface is not Lu Yu’s and is not in the base text, so it is not included in the reading; it is recorded here.

Graphic forms and punctuation. Variant graphs in the base text that carry no difference of meaning have been regularised (eighteen pairs: 隂→陰, 采→採, 淩→凌, 珎→珍, 㪷→斗, 𥥛→突, 昇→升, 䆫→窗 and others). One of the eighteen is a simplified form that had crept in: in Chapter IX the base text alone writes 鹾簋 with the simplified 鹾, while the entry heading in Chapter IV correctly has 鹺簋 — the same kind of thing as the seventeen traces of a round trip through simplified characters found in the base text of the Sanlüe in an earlier round. 葱/蔥 was likewise inconsistent in the base text and has been evened out. The character 鄮 in Chapter VIII, for which the base text substituted an image file, has been given back its Unicode form. The full-width colon 「︰」 has been changed to 「：」, half-width commas and stops to full-width, and eight quotation marks supplied where the base text opened a quotation and never closed it. Apart from the sixteen emendations listed above, not one character of the text has been touched.

The other readings. The traditional and simplified Chinese pages carry, besides the text, a note to each scroll, each chapter and each paragraph, written for this edition and hidden behind the first button in the toolbar. The Japanese page is likewise a complete translation of every paragraph. All four readings share the same paragraph ids, so a link into one lands in the same place in the others.
