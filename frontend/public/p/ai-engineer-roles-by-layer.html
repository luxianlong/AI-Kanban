What this page is for. In 2026 the phrase AI Engineer is attached to at least nine materially different jobs. They share a vocabulary and almost nothing else: different failure modes, different clocks, different interview questions, different hiring bars, different pay curves. Candidates apply across all of them with one résumé and lose. Recruiters screen across all of them with one keyword list and mis-sort.

This article does three things: (1) explains why the confusion is structural rather than anyone's fault, (2) lays out the stack as seven layers plus two cross-cutting columns, with a concrete charter, failure mode and proof artifact for each, and (3) gives you decoders — a 60-second test for locating any job description on the stack, a translation table between the five naming dialects, and a self-location diagnostic.

Time anchor: September 2026. Market observations are drawn from the job-description corpus read for #424 — Together AI, OpenAI, Anthropic, LinkedIn, NVIDIA, Seekr, Databricks, Cloudera — plus the two follow-ups #453 and #454. Where I state a market judgment rather than a quoted requirement, I say so.

0. The answer in one page#

The stack has layers. Job titles do not.

A layer is defined by what you own when it breaks. There are seven of them, from the silicon up to the product surface, plus two columns (data and trust) that cut across all seven. Each layer has its own clock — the time unit in which its characteristic failure happens and must be fixed — and that clock, more than any tool name, is what makes the layers genuinely different jobs.

A title, by contrast, is assigned by the hiring company's own position in the industry, not by the work. The same job — "wire an LLM into a product workflow, with retrieval, tools, evals and a budget" — is called:

  • Member of Technical Staff at a frontier lab,
  • AI Engineer at a Series-B SaaS,
  • Machine Learning Engineer at a bank,
  • Forward-Deployed Engineer at an applied-AI vendor,
  • Senior Full-Stack Engineer (AI) at an agency.

And the reverse: five jobs that are genuinely on five different layers all get posted as "AI Infrastructure Engineer." That is the actual defect. Not that titles are vague — that titles are orthogonal to layers, so a title carries roughly zero bits of information about which job it is.

The practical consequences, which the rest of this article unpacks:

  1. You cannot target "AI roles." You can only target a layer, and then translate your claim into that layer's dialect (§13).
  2. Mobility is asymmetric: it is easy to move up the stack, hard to move down (§14). Most candidates apply downward and get silently filtered.
  3. Pay tracks proximity to the binding constraint — which in 2026 is accelerator capacity, not code (§15). This explains the otherwise strange pay curve.
  4. Layers commoditize upward over time. Some of today's hottest titles are already dissolving into features (§16). Pick a layer by its durability, not its heat.
  5. You can credibly claim your home layer plus one adjacent layer. Claim two away and the phone screen catches you in the first ten minutes (§17).

1. Why the confusion is structural, not sloppy#

It is tempting to conclude that recruiters are careless. They mostly aren't. The incoherence is manufactured by the pipeline that produces a job posting, and it would survive replacing every recruiter in the industry tomorrow.

Four independent sources of drift, each rational on its own:

Source Why it happens What it does to the posting
Levelling ladders are older than the roles Comp bands and titles are approved corporate objects; you cannot invent Agent Runtime Engineer III without an HR cycle Genuinely new work gets filed under the nearest pre-existing title, usually ML Engineer or Software Engineer, AI
Keyword lists are inherited The requisition is cloned from last year's, which was cloned from the MLOps wave Postings carry a tail of skills nobody on the team uses; L6 jobs ask for Kubernetes, L1 jobs ask for PyTorch
The screener has a lag Any model scoring résumés learned the title→skill association from a corpus that predates the current split Titles that meant one thing in 2023 are scored as if they still do
The manager writes in team dialect "Reconciliation loop," "span tree," "continuous batching" — each is a house word somewhere Bullets are precise and layer-specific; the title above them is generic. The two disagree

The single most useful reframe in this article: in a 2026 AI posting, the title is noise and the bullets are signal. Read the bullets, locate the layer (§12 gives you a 60-second method), and then decide whether the title is one you can plausibly wear. Candidates do the reverse — they filter by title first — which is why the funnel is so lossy on both sides.

There is a second, deeper reason, and it is worth saying plainly because it explains why this will not settle down soon: the layers themselves are only a few years old. In 2019 "ML Engineer" was one job because the stack was one layer thick — you owned the model and everything around it. The 2023–2026 build-out split that single role along the seams where the money and the failures concentrate. Naming always lags splitting.

2. The first cut: four prepositions#

Before the seven layers, there is a coarser split that resolves maybe 70% of confusion in one move. Ask what the engineer's relationship to the model is. There are only four answers, and they are prepositions.

Preposition The work Model is... Typical layers Canonical question they answer
Under the model Make the model possible and fast — silicon, fleet, training substrate, serving runtime a workload with a memory and interconnect profile L0, L1, L2, L4 "Why is this job getting 41% MFU instead of 55%?"
Of the model Make the model itself — architecture, data mixture, fine-tuning, RL, evals of capability the artifact you are producing L3 "Did this data-mixture change actually improve the thing we care about?"
With the model Build things that call the model — agents, retrieval, tools, workflows, UX a stochastic API with a price and a latency L6, L7 "Why did this agent take nine tool calls to do a three-call job?"
Around the model Make everything above governable — registries, lineage, evals-as-infrastructure, cost, safety, access an artifact with a version, an owner, a cost and a blast radius L5, plus both columns "Which prompt revision and model build produced this score?"

This cut matters because the four have almost disjoint hiring bars:

  • Under hires on systems depth and is gated by access to hardware you cannot buy at home.
  • Of hires on research taste and is gated by publication or model-shipping history.
  • With hires on product velocity and is gated by almost nothing — which is why it is the most crowded and the fastest-moving.
  • Around hires on distributed-systems maturity and is gated by having operated something real at multi-tenant scale — which is why it skews senior and is the least legible to recruiters.

Most people who say "I want to be an AI engineer" mean with. Most people who say "AI infrastructure" mean under. Most companies that post "AI Platform" mean around but describe it in under's vocabulary. That last mismatch is the single most common mis-sort in the market.

The ordering is dependency, not prestige. L7 depends on L6 depends on L4, and so on down. It is not a ladder to climb — a principal engineer at L7 and a principal engineer at L0 are peers who could not do each other's jobs.

3.1 The master table#

Read this table by column, not by row. Each column is one discriminator: any one of them separates the layers better than a tool list does.

The clock column is the sharpest tool here. Two engineers who both say "I work on inference" but one thinks in microseconds (L0) and the other in milliseconds (L4) are not colleagues; they are supplier and customer. Ask anyone, in any AI job, "what is the time unit of the thing you get paged about?" and you have located them within one layer, without a single tool name changing hands.

4. L0 — Silicon, kernels and interconnect#

Charter. Make the arithmetic go as fast as the hardware allows.

What you own. Fused kernels, attention implementations, quantized matmuls, collective communication schedules, the memory hierarchy, occasionally the network fabric itself.

The characteristic failure. Nothing crashes. The job runs, produces correct results, and is 30% below what the hardware could do — and only someone at this layer can even see that. L0's defining feature is that its failures are invisible to every layer above.

Vocabulary. CUDA, Triton, ROCm, tensor cores, warp specialization, roofline, arithmetic intensity, MFU/HFU, NCCL, RCCL, all-reduce vs. all-gather, NVLink, InfiniBand, RoCE, memory-bound vs. compute-bound, register pressure, shared-memory bank conflicts.

Typical titles. Performance Engineer, GPU · Kernel Engineer · Systems Engineer, ML Performance · Member of Technical Staff (Performance) · sometimes, confusingly, Inference Engineer.

Who hires. Chip vendors, frontier labs, GPU clouds, a handful of inference startups. Small absolute number of roles.

The gate. You cannot practice this layer without the hardware, and you cannot rent enough of it casually to build a portfolio. This is the most hardware-gated layer in the stack and therefore the least penetrable from outside — but also the least crowded.

Proof artifact. A benchmark: baseline, change, measured speedup, and an explanation in terms of the roofline. One credible kernel writeup outweighs any amount of résumé prose.

5. L1 — Fleet and capacity control plane#

Charter. Keep the physical world continuously equal to the declared state: this many healthy nodes, with this driver, in this pool, available to these tenants.

What you own. The lifecycle of a machine modelled as software — discovery, bring-up, driver and firmware installation, health validation, admission to a pool, drain, repair, retirement. Plus the self-service API on top of it, and the quota/scheduling policy that decides who gets the scarce thing.

The characteristic failure. Not "it's down." It's "forty nodes are in the pool, accepting work, and quietly degraded" — an ECC error rate climbing, a NIC negotiating at half rate, a driver that rolled back on reboot. Detecting the gap between declared and actual — drift — is the whole job.

Vocabulary. Provisioning state machine, reconciliation, level-triggered vs. edge-triggered, idempotency, drift detection, self-healing, safe drain, cordon, durable workflow orchestration (Temporal/Cadence), event-driven over queues rather than polling, PXE/Redfish/IPMI, DCGM, node problem detector, bin-packing, preemption, quota, MIG partitioning, zero-downtime rollout.

Typical titles. GPU Infrastructure Engineer · Compute Infrastructure Engineer · Staff SWE, Inference/Compute Infrastructure · Cluster Engineer · Platform Engineer, AI Compute. The Together AI and OpenAI postings in the corpus are canonical examples.

Who hires. GPU clouds, neoclouds, hyperscalers, any company that owns rather than rents its accelerators, and increasingly large enterprises building internal AI capacity.

The interesting property. This is the layer where classical distributed-systems skill transfers almost perfectly — Kubernetes controller patterns, reconcile loops, idempotent retries, queue-driven orchestration. A senior infrastructure engineer from a non-AI background can be productive here in weeks, which is not true of L0, L2 or L3. It is, in other words, the most permeable door into the "under" column, and it is consistently under-applied-to relative to L4, because it has less glamour and the JDs read as ops work.

Proof artifact. A state machine you designed with its failure transitions drawn, and a story about a repair path that ran without a human. See #453 for what an interviewer will actually probe here.

6. L2 — Training platform#

Charter. Make a large distributed training run start, survive, and resume.

What you own. Launcher and scheduler integration, parallelism strategy plumbing (data/tensor/pipeline/expert), checkpointing and restart, data loading throughput, determinism and reproducibility, run observability, the goodput number.

The characteristic failure. A run that costs six figures dies on day four, 80% through, and the checkpoint is either stale or corrupt. Or the run does not die but goodput quietly sits at 60% because of stragglers and restarts.

Vocabulary. DDP, FSDP, DeepSpeed/ZeRO, Megatron-style tensor/pipeline parallelism, activation checkpointing, gradient accumulation, elastic training, checkpoint sharding, async checkpoint offload, Slurm, Ray, goodput, straggler mitigation, NCCL timeouts, loss-spike forensics.

Typical titles. Training Infrastructure Engineer · ML Systems Engineer · Research Infrastructure Engineer · Member of Technical Staff (Training).

Who hires. Labs, model-training startups, a few hyperscaler research orgs. Fewest seats of any layer relative to applicant interest.

The gate. Multi-node, multi-GPU time. You cannot simulate the experience of a 512-GPU run failing in an interesting way. This is why candidates without prior lab or big-tech access essentially cannot enter here, and why the honest advice in #424 was: if you do not already have that time, do not target this layer.

7. L3 — Model production#

Charter. Produce a model that is measurably better at something that matters.

What you own. Architecture and objective choices, data mixture and curriculum, fine-tuning and preference optimization, RL environments and reward design, capability evaluation, ablation discipline.

The characteristic failure. The new checkpoint is worse, and the ablation you needed to explain why was never run. Or: the benchmark improved and the product did not.

Vocabulary. Pretraining, SFT, DPO/GRPO and friends, reward modelling, data mixture, decontamination, scaling laws, ablation, held-out eval, capability elicitation, distillation, LoRA and its limits.

Typical titles. Research Engineer · Research Scientist · Member of Technical Staff · ML Engineer (at companies that only have one AI title) · Applied Scientist.

Who hires. Labs and a shrinking middle of companies who still train their own models. Note the structural trend: as base models improve, fewer organizations do L3 at all; they move the work to L5/L6 (adapt and govern rather than train).

The gate. Research taste, demonstrated by shipped models or published work. This is the one layer where the credential — a paper, a model card with your name on it — is doing real work rather than signalling.

8. L4 — Inference and serving#

Charter. Serve tokens at a target latency for the lowest cost per token.

What you own. The serving runtime and its configuration, batching policy, KV-cache management, quantization strategy, speculative decoding, tensor-parallel sharding for serving, request routing and admission control, autoscaling against GPU utilization, the $/1M-token number.

The characteristic failure. Latency is fine at current traffic and catastrophic at 2×, because the batching policy was tuned for a load shape that changed. Or: the cost curve never bends, and the CFO notices.

Vocabulary. vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM, Triton Inference Server, Ray Serve, continuous (in-flight) batching, paged attention, KV cache and prefix caching, prefill/decode disaggregation, TTFT vs. TPOT, p50/p99, tokens/sec/GPU, INT8/FP8/AWQ/GPTQ, speculative decoding, structured output constraints.

Typical titles. Inference Engineer · Staff AI Infrastructure Engineer (very commonly this layer, despite the generic title) · LLM Serving Engineer · Performance Engineer (overlapping L0).

Who hires. Everyone who serves models at scale: inference providers, GPU clouds, labs, and the growing set of product companies that moved off vendor APIs for cost or latency reasons.

Market note (judgment, not quoted data). This is simultaneously the hottest and most crowded layer. It has the clearest vocabulary, the most public tooling, and the most self-taught applicants — which means the bar is set by people who have genuinely tuned a production fleet, and the filter question comes fast: "walk me through how you chose your batching policy, and what you do when the KV cache saturates." Keyword-stuffing this layer is the most expensive mistake in the market, because the tell is immediate.

9. L5 — The model-consuming platform#

Charter. Make everything above and below addressable, reproducible, attributable and governable — so that an organization can run many models, many prompts and many teams without losing track of what produced what, and at what cost.

What you own. The artifact catalog and model/prompt/dataset registry; lineage and provenance; the evaluation system as infrastructure (not as a script); experiment tracking; the LLM gateway and its routing, quota and budget policy; cost attribution; access control over models and data; the rollout/flighting control plane for AI features.

The characteristic failure. Two failures, both slow and both expensive. First: "which prompt revision and model build produced this score?" and nobody can answer, so a regression cannot be localized. Second: a $400k monthly inference bill that cannot be attributed to a team, a feature or a customer.

Vocabulary. Model/artifact registry, provenance and lineage, dataset and prompt versioning, reproducibility, experiment tracking, offline and online evaluation, LLM-as-judge with human calibration, eval gating in CI, multi-tenancy, quota and budget governance, gateway/router, policy enforcement, drift detection between declared and live configuration, progressive rollout.

Typical titles. AI Platform Engineer · ML Platform Engineer · MLOps Engineer (legacy name, see §16) · Staff SWE, AI Platform · Developer Productivity, AI. LinkedIn's Staff SWE, AI Platform posting in the corpus is a clean example.

Who hires. Any organization with more than one team using models — which by 2026 is most large organizations. This is the largest layer by headcount and the least legible by title.

Why it is chronically mis-sorted. L5's work is distributed systems and metadata, but the posting is written with AI vocabulary, so it attracts L6 applicants (who can write agents but have never operated a multi-tenant service) and repels the senior platform engineers who would be excellent at it (who read "AI Platform" and assume they need PyTorch). If you are a strong backend/data-platform engineer wondering where you fit, this is almost certainly your layer, and the mis-sort is in your favour.

Proof artifact. A registry, lineage graph or eval harness with real tenants and real numbers — artifacts/day, teams served, QPS, p99, cost attributed. The design spec in #423 is an example of the shape.

10. L6 — Agent and application runtime#

Charter. Turn a stochastic API into a system that reliably completes tasks.

What you own. Orchestration and control flow, tool definitions and sandboxing, memory and context management, retrieval, structured output, retry and fallback policy, the span tree of a run, per-run cost and latency, guardrails at the call boundary.

The characteristic failure. The agent does not crash. It loops; it calls nine tools where three would do; it confidently returns something wrong; it costs $4 per run when the unit economics allow $0.40. Every L6 failure is a quality failure, not an availability failure, which is why L6 lives or dies on evaluation.

Vocabulary. Agent loop, tool binding, function calling, MCP, context window management, context compaction, RAG, chunking and reranking, hybrid search, span/trace tree, LangGraph and similar DAG runtimes, multi-agent orchestration, guardrails, deterministic checks vs. statistical metrics vs. LLM-as-judge, token budget per task.

Typical titles. AI Engineer (the modal usage of the bare term) · Agent Engineer · Applied AI Engineer · Forward-Deployed Engineer · Full-Stack Engineer, AI.

Who hires. Nearly everyone. This is the largest and fastest-growing category of posting, and the one with the lowest formal barrier to entry.

The honest warning. Low barrier means the entry is crowded and the senior end is thin. What separates a senior L6 engineer from a hobbyist is never the framework — it is whether they can answer "how do you know it got better?" with an eval suite, a calibration procedure, a cost model and a regression gate. That answer is L5 thinking applied at L6, which is exactly why these two layers are the most natural adjacency pair in the whole stack.

11. L7 — The product surface#

Charter. Make a probabilistic system trustworthy to a human being.

What you own. The interaction model — when to stream, when to show work, when to ask for confirmation, how to present uncertainty, how to fail gracefully, what the undo story is, how feedback gets captured and routed back into evaluation.

The characteristic failure. Everything is technically working and users stop trusting the feature. It is the slowest feedback loop in the stack (weeks of perception), and the only one where the metric is behavioural: acceptance rate, edit distance on suggestions, repeat usage after a bad output.

Vocabulary. Streaming UX, progressive disclosure, human-in-the-loop, confirmation boundaries, uncertainty display, graceful degradation, feedback capture, acceptance rate, prompt affordances, error recovery.

Typical titles. AI Product Engineer · Product Engineer (AI) · Design Engineer · Full-Stack Engineer at companies that do not distinguish.

Underrated point. L7 is the layer most often dismissed as "just frontend," and it is where a surprising share of realized AI value is won or lost. A 15% improvement in model quality and a 15% improvement in how failures are surfaced are worth about the same to the user; the second is far cheaper to obtain.

12. The two columns: Data and Trust#

These are not layers because they do not sit between other things — they touch everything.

Column D — Data#

Acquisition, licensing, curation, deduplication, decontamination, labelling operations, synthetic generation, dataset versioning and contracts, PII handling at the corpus level.

Titles: Data Engineer (AI) · Data Curation Engineer · Research Data Engineer · Annotation/Ops Lead. Historically undervalued, now increasingly recognized as the highest-leverage input at L3, and the most common root cause of a bad L6 system.

The distinguishing question: "What is the version of the dataset, and can you reproduce the model from it?" If nobody can answer, there is no Column D function, regardless of how many data engineers are employed.

Column T — Trust#

Safety and alignment engineering, red-teaming, prompt-injection and agent security, tenant isolation, privacy and retention, model/vendor risk, audit and compliance (EU AI Act and successors), incident response for model behaviour.

Titles: AI Safety Engineer · AI Security Engineer · Responsible AI Engineer · ML Governance Engineer. Two very different flavours hide here: behavioural (does the model do harmful things?) and systems-security (can an attacker make the agent do harmful things?). The second is a genuine new discipline created by L6 — an agent with tool access is a remote-code-execution surface with a natural-language parser in front of it — and demand for it is outrunning supply faster than any other specialty.

13. The 60-second layer test#

Given any job description, ask these five questions in order. You will usually be done in two.

graph TD
  Q1{"Who owns the GPUs?"} -->|"We do, physically"| A1["L0 / L1 / L2 live here"]
  Q1 -->|"A vendor; we call an API"| A2["L5 / L6 / L7 live here"]
  Q1 -->|"We rent instances and run our own serving"| A3["L4 is likely"]
  A1 --> Q2{"Do we TRAIN models?"}
  Q2 -->|"Yes, from scratch or heavy finetune"| B1["L2 / L3"]
  Q2 -->|"No, we run other people's"| B2["L1 / L4"]
  A2 --> Q3{"How many TEAMS consume this?"}
  Q3 -->|"Many teams, we are their platform"| C1["L5"]
  Q3 -->|"One product"| C2["L6 / L7"]
  C2 --> Q4{"Does a user see the output directly?"}
  Q4 -->|"Yes, and we design that moment"| D1["L7"]
  Q4 -->|"It feeds another system"| D2["L6"]

The five questions in plain form, with what each one buys you:

  1. "Who owns the accelerators — you, or a vendor?" Separates under from with/around. This is the highest-information single question in AI hiring.
  2. "Do you train, fine-tune, or call?" Separates L2/L3 from everything else.
  3. "What is the time unit of the thing you get paged about?" Microseconds → L0. Milliseconds → L4. Seconds → L6. Minutes to hours → L1. Days → L2/L3. Nothing pages you, it degrades over quarters → L5 or L7.
  4. "How many teams consume what you build?" One → L6/L7. Many → L5. Nobody, it is the substrate → L1.
  5. "When the base model gets better, does your work get easier or harder?" Easier → you are at L6/L7 (you inherit improvements). Harder → you are at L1/L4 (bigger models, tighter memory, more capacity pressure). Unchanged → L5 (governance need scales with usage, not quality).

Question 5 is the one nobody asks, and it is the most predictive of how durable the role is — see §16.

Three things fall out of this table:

  1. "AI Engineer" unqualified means L6 at a startup and almost nothing anywhere else. If a big-tech posting says AI Engineer, read the bullets; the title was chosen by the ladder, not the manager.
  2. "AI Infrastructure Engineer" is the most overloaded title in the market — it maps to L1, L2, L4 and L5 depending on employer. Four different jobs, one string. The corpus behind #424 contains all four under that exact title.
  3. "MLOps Engineer" is a legacy label covering what is now split between L1 and L5. When an enterprise posts it, it usually means L5 with some L4. When a startup posts it, it usually means "please make our deploys work."

The asymmetry has one clean cause: lower layers are gated by access to hardware and by knowledge that only accrues from operating it; upper layers are gated by judgment that transfers from ordinary software work.

More precisely, three gates compound as you go down:

Gate L6–L7 L5 L4 L1 L2 L0
Can you practice it at home? yes mostly partly barely no no
Does non-AI experience transfer? some almost fully partly almost fully little little
Is the failure mode learnable without production? yes partly no no no no

Note the two anomalies in the transfer row: L5 and L1. Both are, underneath the AI vocabulary, classical distributed-systems jobs — reconcile loops, idempotency, multi-tenancy, queue-driven orchestration, schema and state design. A strong infrastructure engineer with zero AI background is genuinely qualified for both and is usually screened out for lacking GPU keywords. That is the single largest arbitrage in the 2026 AI job market, and it is the reason #424 concluded that the reachable targets for a data-platform engineer were L5 first and L1 second — not L4, however loudly L4 is advertised.

Practical rule. Target your home layer plus one adjacent layer. Two layers away, the phone screen kills you — not on vocabulary, which you can learn in a weekend, but on failure stories, which you cannot fabricate. Every layer's real interview is "tell me about a time this broke," and the shape of a true answer at L4 is nothing like the shape of a true answer at L6.

16. Where the money is, and why#

Pay in this market does not track difficulty, seniority, or even scarcity on its own. It tracks proximity to the binding constraint. In 2026 the binding constraint is accelerator capacity — not engineering talent, and not model quality.

That gives a simple predictor: the closer your work is to making scarce compute produce more value per hour, the better it is paid. Which explains the otherwise odd shape of the curve:

Layer Seats Competition Comp ceiling Why
L0 very few low (hardware-gated) very high Directly multiplies the scarce resource
L1 moderate moderate high Owns whether the scarce resource is usable at all
L2 few very high very high Gated credential; labs bid against each other
L3 few extreme highest Winner-take-most on model quality
L4 many highest high Directly owns $/token; also the most self-taught entrants
L5 most moderate medium-high Cost attribution rather than cost reduction — one step removed
L6 most high at entry, thin at senior medium Value is real but the work is the most substitutable
L7 many medium medium Mis-valued; see §11
Col T growing fast low medium-high Demand created by L6 faster than supply exists

(Seats/competition/ceiling are my qualitative read of the 2026 posting corpus, not survey data. Treat the ordering as the claim, not the levels.)

Two actionable readings:

  • The best risk-adjusted target for an experienced systems engineer is L1 or L5 — moderate competition, high transfer of existing skill, and both are structurally under-applied-to because their postings read as ops or platform work.
  • The best risk-adjusted target for a strong application engineer is L6 + Column T — agent security is a real discipline that barely has practitioners, and it is reachable from L6 rather than from a security background.

17. The escalator: which roles are dissolving#

Layers commoditize upward. Work that is a specialty at one moment becomes a feature of the layer below it, and the specialists either move up or get absorbed.

graph LR
  P2023["2023: Prompt Engineer"] --> P2026["2026: a skill inside L6/L7,
not a job"]
  R2024["2024: RAG Engineer"] --> R2026["2026: a component of L6;
much of it is now a product feature"]
  M2021["2021: MLOps Engineer"] --> M2026["2026: split into L1 and L5"]
  S2024["2024: per-company serving team"] --> S2026["2026: increasingly a vendor;
survives only where cost or latency justifies it"]
  E2026["2026: Evaluation Engineer"] --> E2028["next: absorbed into L5 as infrastructure,
the way CI absorbed 'build engineer'"]

The pattern is consistent enough to use as a forecasting tool. A role is dissolving when: (a) its core artifact ships as a product feature by a vendor, (b) its distinguishing knowledge becomes a paragraph in documentation, and (c) the base model improving makes it easier rather than harder. That last is question 5 from §13, and it is the early warning.

A role is durable when it sits on top of a constraint that does not go away:

  • L1 — physical capacity is permanently scarce and permanently messy. Durable.
  • L5 — governance load grows with usage, not with model quality. Better models create more artifacts to track, not fewer. Structurally durable.
  • Column T — adversaries adapt; compliance ratchets. Durable.
  • L6 — durable as a function, substitutable as a person, unless you own the evaluation and cost discipline that makes it engineering rather than assembly.
  • L7 — durable; human trust does not commoditize.

The counterintuitive conclusion: the loudest roles (L4, L6 entry) are the ones most exposed to commoditization, and the quietest ones (L1, L5, Column T) are the most durable. Heat and durability are close to anti-correlated in this market, because heat is generated by how easy a role is to talk about, and ease of talking about it is exactly what makes it easy to productize.

18. Positioning: the two-layer rule#

Everything above converges on one practical procedure.

Step 1 — Find your home layer honestly. Not the layer you want; the layer whose failure stories you actually own. Use the clock test on yourself: what is the time unit of the thing that has woken you up?

Step 2 — Pick exactly one adjacent layer to reach for. Adjacent means one step on the stack diagram, or one step across into a column. Your résumé then makes a two-layer claim: home layer with evidence, adjacent layer with one credible artifact.

Step 3 — Translate into the target employer's dialect using §14. Same evidence, different words. This is not spin; it is the fact that L5 at LinkedIn and L5 at a bank genuinely use different house vocabulary for the same machine.

Step 4 — Say your layer out loud in the first line. The single highest-value edit available to most AI-adjacent résumés is replacing a generic summary opener with a sentence that names the layer and the artifact. Recruiters cannot infer your layer, and the screener will not try.

What breaks this:

  • Claiming a layer whose vocabulary you have but whose failures you don't. The tell is instant and unrecoverable. Better to say "I have not run this, here is the nearest thing I have run" — which is a credible senior answer — than to be caught.
  • Claiming two layers away, hoping the adjacent one carries you. It doesn't; the interviewer probes the far claim first, because it is the interesting one.
  • Listing tools instead of failures. Every layer's skills section looks the same from outside. The failure story is what is layer-specific and non-fakeable.

19. Worked example: locating one real résumé#

The three pages preceding this one worked on a single concrete case. It is a good illustration because it contains the two most common mis-sorts in the market at once.

The profile. Eleven years of distributed systems; seven at Microsoft, on data governance and provenance; tech lead of a self-built multi-tenant graph database; owned lineage in Microsoft Purview end to end; recently extended the same provenance layer to models, prompts, agents and evaluation runs; built an agent evaluation harness with span trees and LLM-as-judge scoring; built a rollout/flighting control plane and an AI-assisted tool that inventories and retires stale rollout configuration.

Layer location:

Evidence Layer
Artifact catalog, lineage, provenance, multi-tenancy, experiment tracking L5, strong
Agent eval harness, span-tree tracing, LLM-judge calibration L6, real but recent
Feature-flight rollout control plane, drift detection, zero-downtime rebuild L1-shaped, adjacent
Online feature retrieval for model serving (2016) L5/L4 border, dated
GPU, CUDA, batching, KV cache nothing

So: home layer L5, adjacent reach L6, second adjacency L1. Exactly the "moderate competition, high transfer, structurally under-applied-to" profile from §15 — and exactly not L4, which is what a résumé aimed at the phrase "AI Infrastructure" would be scored against.

The two mis-sorts this case demonstrates:

  1. Aiming at the loud layer. "Staff AI Infrastructure Engineer" reads as L4 in most markets; the evidence here is L5. Same title, three layers apart. The fix is not to learn L4 vocabulary — it is to target the postings under that title that are actually L5, which §14's table lets you identify from the bullets.
  2. Labelling evidence with the wrong layer's vocabulary. #453 examined six control-plane terms on the skills line and found the underlying skills genuinely present; #454 then found that the label was claiming an L1 job ("I keep a machine converged, forever") while the evidence was an L5 migration story ("I moved a large stateful system without downtime, once"). Both are respectable. They are different layers, they get different interview questions, and the mismatch was costing more than the keywords were earning.

Note what this example is really showing: the layer model is not just a taxonomy of the market — it is a consistency check on your own document. A résumé is coherent when the title, the summary, the skills line and the bullets all point at the same layer. Most aren't, and the incoherence is invisible to the author because they know what they meant.

20. If you are the recruiter#

The premise of the question this article answers was that recruiters often are not clear on the distinction. Fair — but as §1 argues, the pipeline produces that. Three changes fix most of it, and they are cheap:

  1. Put the clock in the posting. One sentence: "You will be paged about p99 latency on token generation" or "You will own whether 4,000 GPUs are healthy" or "You will own why an evaluation score cannot be reproduced." This does more sorting than the entire requirements list, and it costs one line.
  2. State who owns the accelerators. "We operate our own fleet" vs. "we consume vendor APIs" is the highest-information fact about an AI engineering job and it is almost never stated.
  3. Screen on failure stories, not tool names. Ask "tell me about a time the system you owned degraded" and listen for the time unit and blast radius in the answer. A candidate who answers in milliseconds and a candidate who answers in quarters are both possibly excellent, and only one of them fits the role.

A useful diagnostic for a posting you already have: if you deleted the title and the tool names, could an engineer still tell which layer it is? If not, the posting is going to produce a mis-sorted funnel no matter how many applicants it attracts.

21. A self-location diagnostic#

Ten questions. Answer from what you have done, not what you have read.

# Question If yes, points to
1 Have you made something measurably faster by changing how memory is accessed? L0
2 Have you written the code that decides when a machine is healthy enough to receive work? L1
3 Have you resumed a distributed job from a checkpoint after a hardware failure? L2
4 Have you run an ablation that changed a decision about a model? L3
5 Have you tuned a batching or caching policy and watched p99 and $/token move? L4
6 Have you built something that many other teams depend on and file bugs against? L5
7 Can you answer "how do you know it got better?" with a suite rather than a vibe? L5 or L6 (senior)
8 Have you debugged an agent that did the wrong thing confidently? L6
9 Have you changed how a model's output is presented and seen behaviour change? L7
10 Have you found a way to make a system with tool access do something it shouldn't? Column T

Your home layer is where you have two or more yeses with production stories behind them. Your reach layer is where you have exactly one. Anything with zero is not a target this year — it is a project plan.

If you got yeses scattered across 1, 5 and 8 with nothing in between, you have a common and awkward profile: broad exposure, no home. The fix is to pick the one with the best story and deepen it until it has two more; breadth without a home layer reads as junior regardless of years served.

22. Summary: the nine sentences worth keeping#

  1. The stack has layers; titles don't. A title tells you about the employer, not the job.
  2. A layer is defined by what breaks and how fast, not by which tools are installed.
  3. Four prepositions do most of the sorting: under, of, with, around the model.
  4. "AI Infrastructure Engineer" covers four different layers; "AI Engineer" unqualified means L6 at a startup; "MLOps" is a legacy label for what is now L1 plus L5.
  5. Read the bullets, not the title — then use the clock test to place them.
  6. Mobility is asymmetric: up the stack is weeks, down the stack is quarters or blocked.
  7. L1 and L5 are the arbitrage: classical distributed-systems jobs wearing AI vocabulary, systematically under-applied-to by exactly the people qualified for them.
  8. Heat and durability are anti-correlated: the easiest roles to talk about are the easiest to productize.
  9. Claim your home layer plus one. Vocabulary is free; failure stories are not.
