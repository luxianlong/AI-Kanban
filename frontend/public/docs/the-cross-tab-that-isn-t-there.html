Nine documents, 156 pages, 2022 to 2025, all from one vendor's resource hub. Read in order they look like a single argument getting louder: identify, classify, protect; then identify, classify, protect, for AI. The corpus's weakest argument is the one it leads with, its strongest evidence is treated as background texture, and its most consequential idea appears once, in a sentence nobody follows up.

Every number the corpus originates is vendor-funded; the rest is rented. The Data Security Index (2024) is a Microsoft-commissioned Hypothesis Group survey: 20 minutes online, 5–23 August 2024, n=1,376, CISO and adjacent decision-makers (C-2 and above) with purview over data security, 500+ employees, six countries, education/government/nonprofit excluded. Top 3 Challenges in Securing and Governing Data for the Era of AI (04/25) is a Hypothesis synthesis over four Microsoft studies — the Secure and Govern AI whitepaper (MDC Research, "over 400"), the Index itself, Audience Research (MDC, four focus groups plus 600), and Customer Requirements Research (Hypothesis, 400, US/UK only) — plus rented figures from Forbes, Statista, Precisely, Dataversity, the Data Leaders Guild and Cybersecurity Ventures. The IDC white paper (#US53090324, April 2025) carries "sponsored by Microsoft" in every footer, though its one quantitative exhibit is IDC's own survey. Sponsorship is not disqualifying. The test is internal consistency. Where a gap runs consistently in the sponsor's favor I will note the direction, which is a pattern and not a motive.

The confound is printed beside the claim#

The load-bearing claim is that tool sprawl causes incidents. The Index states it as correlation — "In 2024, organizations using more data security tools (11 or more) experienced an average of 202 data security incidents, compared to 139 incidents for those with 10 or fewer tools." The 2025 paper reproduces the chart and hardens the framing: "often, the more tools an organization uses, the more incidents they experience."

The same two-column spread that carries that chart also reports that medium enterprises run 9 tools, large 11, extra-large 14 — and that medium and large organizations see 89 incidents a year against 248 for extra-large. Tool count and headcount are collinear by the report's own figures, which makes the marginal contrast uninterpretable in either direction. A real independent tool effect, pure proxying for size, and everything between all produce the printed numbers.

The report cuts by employee band constantly: alerts per day (56 versus 80), AI-app breach rates (36/38/44%), concern about missing AI controls (43/50/54%), expected increases in AI-related incidents. It never publishes tool count against incidents within a band. That cut would not rescue the causal claim — "incident" is never defined, so instrumentation plausibly drives the measurement; organizations that keep getting hit keep buying; a single-wave cross-sectional self-report separates none of this. What the cut would do is remove the one confounder the report hands you itself. Whatever the reason it is missing, the position is the same: a vendor made a causal claim, holds the data that would discipline it, and published the cuts that support the pitch.

The preference data is weaker. "Most decision-makers (82%) agree that a comprehensive, fully integrated platform is superior to managing multiple isolated tools," which the Final Recommendations convert into "the move toward consolidation isn't just beneficial — it's essential." The item names no cost — not detection quality, not vendor concentration, not migration — so agreement is nearly free, and a free item separates nobody. The same respondents are "juggling 12 different data security solutions." That divergence is the interesting part, and it has more candidate explanations than the corpus entertains: mid-migration estates, acquisitions, regulator- or customer-mandated tools, deliberate best-of-breed policy, integrated platforms not yet covering what point tools cover, or switching costs. Nothing here distinguishes them, and I cannot either from these documents. By 2025 the items have stopped discriminating altogether: 95% agree unification is a priority (Audience Research, n=600), over 90% say their organization will adopt a unified solution (Customer Requirements, n=400, US/UK). When one-off agreement items return 90–95%, that is a fact about question design, not a trajectory. The 82% itself reappears in the 2025 paper de-numbered — "the majority of organizations believe that a comprehensive approach with integrated solutions is superior" — still footnoted to the Index, a number becoming a vaguer assertion while gaining the authority of repetition.

One more. "Organizations that have implemented AI to strengthen data security see a 65% reduction in data security incidents compared to those not using AI." The verb does work the design cannot support: a between-group difference described as a within-group change, with nobody measured before and after. The companion figure — 47 alerts a day for AI adopters against 79 — is presented as unambiguously good, but fewer alerts is also what tuning down detection looks like, and the metric that would separate the readings, incidents per reviewed alert, is not reported.

What survives#

The operational material, which nobody pull-quoted. Alerts average 66 a day, up from 52 in 2023, reaching 80 at 5,000+ employees; teams review 63% of them; "Thirty-five percent of these alerts turn out to be false positives." The antecedent of "these" is ambiguous — all daily alerts, or the reviewed 63% — and it is the difference between a fifth and a third of the queue being reviewed noise. Either way these are questions a security leader can answer about his own week without depending on attribution.

The severity split is the other real finding: 27% of incidents severe in 2024 against 20% in 2023. The volume trend beside it is not usable — 166 to 156 compares a wave of over 800 respondents to one of over 1,300, with an undefined outcome — but the within-wave severity share stands on its own. Shadow AI stands too, not because self-report is reliable but because its bias has a known direction: 65% admit unauthorized employee AI use, broken out into personal credentials, personal devices and work credentials on personal errands, all within six points of each other. You cannot report shadow use you have not detected, so 65% is a floor. And the top-five needed AI controls land at 43/42/42/42/41%, perfectly flat. Nobody has a dominant strategy. The report presents it as a priority ranking.

The rented numbers travel worse. "New regulatory requirements are on the rise due to AI" is supported by "over 200 daily updates across over 900 regulatory agencies," footnoted to Thomson Reuters via Forbes, 2021 — a pre-ChatGPT figure under a post-ChatGPT causal clause. Enterprise data "grow[ing] over 40% year over year" is sourced to a Statista series covering 2020–2022. And IDC's single exhibit does not reconcile with its own chart: "over 85% of financial institutions expect at least 3x ROI for every dollar spent on GenAI projects (with 35% expecting more than 4x ROI)" against Figure 1's 3x 50%, 4x 21%, 5x 15%, above-5x 3%. At least 3x is 89%, so the 85% is defensible; more than 4x is 18% and 4x-and-above is 39%, so the 35% is not. The sample, n=450, is IDC's AP Data, AI, GenAI and Insights Survey — an Asia-Pacific base in a paper framed on U.S. Treasury guidance and the EU AI Act, a scope the text never mentions. The same standard has to run toward the sentence I am about to lean on: it is footnote 9, the US/UK-only survey of 400 tool users.

The catalog tradition changes owners#

Read in publication order, the nine record a category redrawing its boundary.

The 2022 e-book is the control sample. Across ten pages, lineage, catalog, steward and data quality appear zero times. "Governance" appears three times: in a footnote to an Enterprise Strategy Group report, once naming an adjacent department — the bolt-on approach forces "security, data governance, compliance, and legal teams to stitch together a patchwork" — and once as the closing call to action, "Go beyond governance." Its two commissioned studies surveyed 512 US compliance decision-makers (Vital Findings, September 2021) and 200 more (MDC, February 2022). By August 2024 the screen reads CISO and C-2 and above. The commissioned respondent pool moved and the framing moved with it — though the 2022 text already addresses "CIOs and CISOs," and the studies had different purposes, so which moved first is not observable here.

The 2024 Crash Course in Microsoft Purview is the honest middle case: twelve steps across three parts, and the e-book never once shows an output of the Data Map half (Atlas APIs, catalog, access policies) feeding any step in the labels/DLP/insider-risk half or the records/eDiscovery/lifecycle half. The nearest thing to a seam is Part 3's lifecycle step, which consumes classification from Part 2, not the map from Part 1. Absence from the narration is not absence from the product; what it shows is how the vendor chose to narrate a brand stapled together from Azure Purview and the Microsoft 365 compliance suite. The same year, the 22-page flagship research instrument asks nothing about catalogs, lineage, stewardship or data quality. Zero mentions. In 2024 those were not the security buyer's problem.

By April 2025 the boundary is gone, in one sentence reporting where buyers said a unified platform would create the most value: "leaders cite a need for data security teams to gain visibility into user risk profiles for all data accessed in a data catalog." The catalog is not the product there. It is a source, with user risk telemetry flowing along the arrow. The paper's diagram makes it visual — Data Security Investigations, Privacy Risk Management, Data Loss Prevention, and Data Quality, the one data-management function admitted as a peer, justified in the adjacent text on output trust ("It is no longer enough to keep your data secure; organizations must activate quality data") and earlier by "data generated by AI cannot be trusted without data quality measures in place." Lineage gets no seat: pictured as a key step in 2023, one word in a list in 2024, absent from the 2025 flagship. The shipping catalog still renders lineage; these documents are only where a vendor decides which capabilities carry the buying argument, and lineage stopped carrying it. Meanwhile the state-of-practice evidence in that paper is all rented — 62% lacking strong governance from Precisely, 25% with a global data quality program from the Data Leaders Guild, 70% planning a federated model from Dataversity. Microsoft's instruments supply the buyer-preference half; the practice half comes from data-management vendors.

Why retrieval makes this an engineering problem#

None of the documents states the mechanism, so here it is. The label model assumes sensitivity is a durable attribute of a byte range that survives transformation because transformations preserve structure. The 2022 e-book says it normatively: copy credit card numbers from Word into Excel and "the classification and policies should automatically apply to both documents." Copy, move, attach, convert — all structure-preserving, and label inheritance as max-of-inputs is roughly right.

Inference is not structure-preserving, and max-of-inputs is wrong for it in both directions. Summarize the public boilerplate of a Highly Confidential memo and the output inherits the maximum and is over-classified into uselessness. Join twenty individually General records and the output's sensitivity exceeds every input — aggregation and re-identification, which has no representation anywhere in the corpus. Sensitivity becomes a property of the tuple (caller, retrieval set at time T, prompt), and there is no object to hang a label on.

That reframes the corpus's own confidence numbers. Eighty-eight percent "believe they know where most of their critical information resides" and 85% "say their data is properly classified and labeled" are measurements of executive confidence, not of protection. The report says so itself: "a disconnect between decision-makers' confidence... and the true level of protection." The financial-services e-book lists misclassified data and inappropriate access permissions among the causes of oversharing. Those were latent defects: an over-permissioned site was survivable because exploiting it required a human who knew the file existed and went looking. Retrieval searches everything the caller can technically read and surfaces it in answer to a question the caller didn't know how to ask. Residual classification error used to be dormant; now it is queryable in natural language.

Lineage changes character rather than extending. The old graph inverts: deterministic transforms, column-level semantics, impact analysis by walking upstream. An inference edge has fan-in of the entire retrieval corpus or training set and no invertible mapping. You can record the edge; you cannot answer "which outputs are contaminated by this source," the only question that matters after a poisoning incident. IDC is the only document that develops an integrity threat model — the Index gestures at it in one line about attacks that "compromise the functionality of the AI systems themselves" — citing Treasury's 2024 report under EO 14110 on "data poisoning, leakage, and integrity attacks," and elsewhere naming "unauthorized model tampering" and misuse of generated content. Its remedy is cloud modernization, where "platformwide encryption, IAM, and real-time threat detection" are said to mitigate "data breaches, algorithmic bias, model tampering, and explainability challenges." Those controls answer the first item and none of the other three. Encryption is not a bias control and IAM is not an explainability control; the mitigation sentence does not address most of the threats in its own list.

And the principal set is missing. Across all nine documents "agent" appears twice, both times meaning endpoint software: an "endpoint detection and response (EDR) agent," and a case study praising DLP "without deploying agents." IDC's IAM section is framed entirely around humans accessing "resources from various locations and devices." But RBAC and ABAC both presuppose a principal set that is enumerable, reasonably stable, and analyzable when the policy is written, and an agent population is none of those. The question stops being "may this principal read this object" and becomes "may this chain, at this depth, with this accumulated delegation." Confused deputy is not a new vulnerability class; running it at machine rate against a principal set you cannot list is new. One Zero Trust pillar does not survive the transition: least privilege is defined against a known task, and an agent's task arrives at runtime, in a prompt. Assume-breach generalizes cleanly — per-invocation blast-radius containment, audit keyed to the invocation.

The test#

The defensible consolidation argument was never "fewer tools, fewer incidents." It is that correlating signal across systems with no shared identity, classification or asset key is expensive, the cost lands on humans, and it shows up in the queue: a third of alerts never opened, a large share of what is opened false. That is a claim about join cost, and it is evaluable without a survey. The Index nearly says it — when tools don't integrate, teams "have to build processes to correlate data and establish a cohesive view of risks."

Which is why the catalog sentence matters, and it matters despite being exactly what I spent a section discrediting: a stated-preference item from an n=400 US/UK vendor survey of tool users. The point is not that leaders want it. The point is that honoring it commits the vendor to an architecture the corpus never costs out. "User risk profiles for all data accessed in a data catalog" is, if you build it, a join: an identity-risk score against the transitive closure of access paths in a catalog graph. That relocates lineage from documentation artifact to access-control input, and design-time, manually curated lineage is then not merely incomplete but unsound — an edge you didn't capture is a permission you didn't enforce. Neither Atlas's model nor OpenLineage's run/job/dataset facets has a first-class node for a principal, a prompt, a model version or a response, and neither carries the identity join. Nobody in these nine documents states a latency budget, a freshness bound, or fail-open-versus-fail-closed semantics for a metadata service in a query's critical path. A batch-scanned system of record and an online policy input are different systems with different SLOs, and the pitch treats them as the same box.

So here is a marker, and it should be applied evenly. Lineage in this category is currently a rendering — a graph you look at during an investigation. It becomes infrastructure when a policy can be expressed over it: when a retrieval or DLP rule can say "deny any output derived from an asset in the transitive closure of this node," and that predicate evaluates inline, against a graph with incremental closure maintenance, a stated staleness bound, and a defined behavior when it is unreachable. By that test nothing I know of qualifies, the engine side included. Warehouse-native catalogs derive column lineage from execution and evaluate tag-based masking and row policies at query time, which is closer than a scanner-built graph, but tag propagation on write is not a closure predicate, and I am not aware of any system that exposes one as a policy primitive with a staleness contract attached.

What remains is a genuinely open bet with two durable advantages on opposite sides. Sitting on the enforcement path buys lineage completeness as a byproduct of execution and the only place a predicate can be evaluated in time to matter. Owning the CISO's budget, the endpoints, the mail and the Copilot traffic buys estate coverage, which is most of the surface and none of the moment the query runs. These nine documents place the whole wager on breadth and assemble survey evidence for it. The wager will not be settled by survey evidence; it will be settled the first time one of these products ships a policy language in which a closure predicate is a first-class term, and publishes what happens when the graph is stale.
